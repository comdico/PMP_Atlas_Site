    Project Charter

    What a project is

    A project is a temporary effort undertaken to create a unique product, service, result, capability, or organizational change. That compact definition has practical consequences. A project is not simply “work with a deadline,” and it is not routine operations with a new label. It exists to move a customer or organization from a current condition toward a different future condition under uncertainty.

    Temporary means the project has a beginning and an ending condition. It may last weeks or years; temporary does not mean short. The service, building, system, or organizational capability created by the project may continue long after the project team has disbanded. The temporary organization therefore needs a clear mandate, a point at which authority begins, and conditions under which the effort ends or transitions.

    Unique means that the result or change is not merely repetitive operational production. Similar projects can use the same lifecycle, templates, suppliers, or technical patterns, but their objectives, stakeholders, constraints, environment, and decisions are not identical. That uniqueness prevents the organization from relying entirely on routine operating authority and procedures.

    Projects are also change-oriented. They introduce a capability, meet an obligation, solve a problem, test an opportunity, or otherwise alter a current state. Change creates affected stakeholders, competing expectations, adoption needs, and resistance as well as technical work. A project can produce its outputs successfully and still fail to create the intended change if those factors are ignored.

    Projects are constrained and uncertain. They consume limited time, money, people, equipment, data, and management attention while depending on assumptions, agreements, technology, regulation, and other parties. Some uncertainty is a threat; some is an opportunity. Authorization must therefore establish enough boundary and authority to begin responsible planning without pretending that every requirement, estimate, risk, or solution detail is already known.

    Finally, a project is value-oriented. Someone expects a benefit, capability, compliance outcome, learning result, risk reduction, or other justified improvement. That expected value explains why scarce organizational resources should be committed. The combination of temporary authority, unique change, uncertainty, constraints, and expected value is why a project needs an explicit authorization rather than an informal instruction to “get started.”

    Why authorization is necessary

    A project charter is the formal authorization record that changes an initiative from a proposal or idea into a recognized project or phase. It authorizes project existence, connects the work to a business need, opportunity, obligation, or strategy; identifies the sponsor or other authorizing authority; recognizes project manager authority where applicable; and defines the initial limits within which organizational resources may be coordinated.

    The charter is therefore not an administrative cover sheet. Its approval establishes that an authorized party has considered the reason for the effort, accepts the high-level exposure and resource commitment, and supports detailed planning within stated boundaries. The project manager receives legitimate coordination and escalation authority, while functional, financial, procurement, compliance, product, and acceptance authorities retain the decisions assigned to them.

    Planning before authorization can be exploratory and useful, but planning without authorization is not the same as authorized project planning. If teams begin making supplier commitments, assigning people, promising dates, or elaborating scope without a mandate, they risk wasted analysis, unsupported resource use, conflicting expectations, and an inability to resolve decisions. A clear charter gives planning a legitimate starting point and a shared reference when later requests drift from the original purpose.

    Approval does not authorize every future decision. It authorizes the project’s existence and the project manager’s initial work within defined limits. Detailed baselines, contracts, releases, controlled changes, technical verification, customer acceptance, and operational transition still require their own evidence and authorities.

    What the charter contains and why

    Business need or justification

    The business need explains why the initiative deserves consideration: a service problem, strategic objective, legal obligation, opportunity, capability gap, or unacceptable risk. It belongs in the charter because authorization without a reason is merely permission to spend resources. The charter summarizes the justification and normally draws on a fuller Business Case; it does not replace the alternatives, feasibility, cost, risk, and value analysis in that case.

    Purpose, measurable objectives, and success criteria

    The purpose describes the intended change in language that stakeholders can understand. Objectives convert that intention into results that can be assessed. “Improve customer service” is an aspiration; an objective identifies the expected improvement, boundary, and time horizon without manufacturing certainty that is not yet available.

    Success criteria explain how authorized stakeholders will judge the result. Project-management success—such as meeting an approved milestone or financial boundary—is related to but distinct from product success and benefits realization. A project may deliver an accepted portal on time while adoption or service-time benefits emerge later under an operational benefit owner.

    High-level scope, exclusions, requirements, and deliverables

    High-level scope establishes the broad work and outcomes inside the authorized initiative and the boundaries outside it. It remains high-level because detailed scope planning—including requirements elicitation, scope definition, decomposition, backlog refinement, and estimating—follows authorization. Excessive detail would freeze assumptions before the team has investigated them; too little detail would permit incompatible interpretations of what was approved.

    High-level requirements record capabilities, conditions, and obligations already known to matter—for example accessibility, retention, security, or a required service outcome. They are not a claim that all requirements are known. Major deliverables translate the initiative into principal outputs or results that can later be decomposed, verified, accepted, and transitioned.

    Exclusions are equally important. They prevent reasonable but unauthorized expectations from silently entering the project. An exclusion does not mean the need has no value; it means that the current authorization does not include it and that adding it requires the appropriate decision.

    Summary milestones and financial authorization

    Summary milestones identify consequential target points, external commitments, governance reviews, or required transitions without pretending to be a detailed schedule. They help the sponsor judge feasibility and urgency and give planning a temporal boundary from which dependencies and estimates can be developed.

    The summary financial authorization identifies an expected funding amount, range, or limit sufficient for the authorization decision. It is not necessarily the cost baseline developed during planning. The distinction matters: a sponsor can authorize an initiative within a financial boundary while requiring detailed estimates, contingency analysis, time phasing, funding reconciliation, and any management-reserve decisions to follow the organization’s planning and governance process.

    High-level risks, assumptions, and constraints

    High-level risks expose uncertainties significant enough to affect authorization. A threat might make the initiative infeasible or unacceptable; an opportunity might increase value or justify discovery. The charter is not the complete Risk Register. Its job is to make the authorization decision honest and to carry major uncertainty into later identification and analysis.

    Assumptions state what is being treated as true despite incomplete evidence, such as supplier capacity or availability of operational data. Recording them prevents fragile beliefs from masquerading as facts and creates input for the Assumption Log, risk work, and validation actions. Constraints are different: they limit options, such as a statutory deadline, fixed funding ceiling, mandated platform, contract term, or scarce specialist resource.

    Key stakeholders, sponsor, and assigned project manager

    The charter names or recognizes stakeholders essential to authorization, governance, value, impact, or acceptance. Their presence shows whose interests and authority shaped the high-level decision. It does not replace the broader Stakeholder Register, which is developed iteratively and includes affected people who may have little formal power.

    The sponsor connects the project to organizational direction, secures support, and exercises the authorizing or escalation authority assigned by governance. Naming the project manager or lead matters because someone must integrate planning, coordinate contributors, maintain decision traceability, and route matters beyond delegated authority. The proposed project manager may help develop the charter, but does not normally self-authorize the project.

    Project-manager authority

    The authority statement explains what the project manager may do after approval. It may include coordinating planning, requesting or applying approved resources, making project decisions within stated thresholds, convening stakeholders, maintaining project information, and escalating unresolved decisions. It should also reveal important limits: the project manager is not automatically the sponsor, product owner, functional manager, procurement authority, compliance authority, change authority, quality verifier, or customer acceptance authority.

    Authority is meaningful only when it is matched to the organization. A charter cannot grant rights the sponsor does not possess, override law or contracts, or silently take people away from functional work. Where resource managers, governance boards, or product authorities retain decisions, the charter should make those interfaces visible.

    Approval requirements, exit criteria, and authorization evidence

    Approval requirements identify important high-level decisions or acceptance conditions that later work must satisfy. Exit or termination criteria describe circumstances under which the project or phase may be completed, transitioned, suspended, cancelled, or returned for reauthorization. They reduce the tendency to continue spending simply because work has already begun.

    Authorization evidence—such as an approved record, signature, recorded governance decision, or equivalent controlled confirmation—changes status. The exact mechanism is tailored, but ambiguity is dangerous: stakeholders should be able to tell whether the project is proposed, approved for discovery, authorized for delivery, or awaiting a reserved decision.

    What the charter does not contain

    The charter establishes authorization and high-level boundaries. It normally does not replace detailed requirements documentation, the detailed project scope statement, a Work Breakdown Structure, the schedule baseline, the cost baseline, the Risk Register, the Stakeholder Register, subsidiary plans, the complete Project Management Plan, backlog decomposition, Sprint planning, detailed procurement documents, or operating procedures.

    These distinctions protect the sequence of reasoning. The Business Case asks whether investment is justified. The charter authorizes the project and initial authority. The Project Management Plan explains how the authorized work will be executed, monitored, controlled, and closed. The project scope statement and scope baseline progressively define and control detailed scope. A product backlog may elaborate and order product work, but neither a backlog nor a Sprint Goal supplies organizational project authorization.

    How the charter is created

    Chartering begins when a need, opportunity, compliance obligation, strategy, or customer commitment creates a credible reason for change. The sponsor or initiating authority reviews the business case, benefits information, agreements, current conditions, feasibility evidence, organizational policies, and known constraints. Enterprise conditions influence what is possible; organizational process assets provide policies, templates, lessons, historical information, and governance practices. Neither category authorizes the project by itself.

    The sponsor, proposed project manager, business and benefit owners, customer representatives, functional leaders, subject-matter experts, and commercial or legal authorities contribute according to the initiative. They clarify the desired future state, negotiate high-level boundaries, test assumptions, identify major stakeholders and uncertainty, and distinguish decisions that can be delegated from decisions reserved to governance.

    A realistic sequence is:

    1. State the need, opportunity, obligation, or strategic objective and identify who can sponsor the decision.
    2. Review the Business Case, expected benefits, feasibility, agreements, known constraints, and source evidence.
    3. Involve key decision-makers and affected experts to clarify purpose, outcomes, high-level scope, requirements, milestones, funding, risks, and assumptions.
    4. Name the project manager or lead and negotiate usable authority, resource interfaces, approval expectations, and escalation routes.
    5. Resolve material ambiguity or return the proposal for further evidence when feasibility, funding, authority, or risk remains unacceptable.
    6. Have the sponsor or authorized governance body approve, reject, defer, or conditionally authorize the charter.
    7. Communicate the decision and begin detailed planning from the approved boundaries.

    The exact record and sequence depend on governance. A contract or statement of work can supply important commitments and, in some contexts, may function as authorization, but an external agreement does not automatically settle internal authority, resource allocation, or governance.

    Lifecycle behavior

    Before approval, the charter is a proposal. It becomes effective when the designated authority approves it under the governing method. Planning then elaborates its objectives and boundaries into management strategies, requirements, scope, schedules, costs, risk responses, stakeholder engagement, communications, procurement, quality, resources, and delivery detail.

    The charter remains a reference throughout the project. When a request appears urgent but unrelated to the original need, or when success criteria become contested, the team can return to the authorization record. This does not make the charter a routine status report or a detailed baseline. It preserves the rationale and limits against which later decisions are interpreted.

    The charter is normally controlled as an authorization record rather than routinely rewritten for every minor change. A material change to purpose, sponsorship, funding authority, strategic alignment, or fundamental boundaries may require sponsor or governance action, reauthorization, suspension, or cancellation. Approved detailed changes generally belong in the relevant plan, baseline, backlog, contract, or project document under its own control process.

    At closure, the charter remains historical evidence of the original mandate. It helps explain what was authorized, how the project’s direction evolved, and whether the completed or terminated effort satisfied the conditions for closure or transition.

    Delivery approach differences

    In a predictive context, the charter normally precedes detailed planning and the establishment of baselines. It supplies enough definition to authorize that planning while deliberately leaving detailed scope, schedule, and cost development to later processes.

    In an adaptive context, authorization, purpose, funding, stakeholders, constraints, and decision authority still matter. The charter or equivalent authorization may be shorter, outcome-oriented, and less prescriptive about detailed solution scope. It may authorize discovery, a product initiative, or an initial release while allowing requirements and design to emerge through feedback. Scrum does not prescribe a project charter, and the Scrum accountabilities do not replace organizational sponsorship or funding governance.

    In a Kanban context, work may enter an ongoing service system rather than a newly formed project. If the initiative is governed as a project, the authorization still needs to define its purpose, boundaries, funding, and authority; Kanban’s flow practices do not supply that mandate. The form should follow the actual governance model rather than forcing every service improvement into a project document.

    In a hybrid context, the charter can establish predictive governance—funding, contracts, compliance obligations, milestone expectations, acceptance authority, and release boundaries—while adaptive teams elaborate detailed product scope iteratively. The charter protects the integrated commitment without pretending that the entire product backlog is known at authorization.

    ServiceBridge worked example

    Authorization record

    • Project: ServiceBridge Modernization
    • Sponsor: Chief Customer Officer
    • Project manager: Service Modernization Program Manager

    Business need: Northstar Public Services relies on fragmented customer-service and appointment processes that create delay, inconsistent accessibility, and weak record traceability. Modernization is needed to improve service access while meeting retention, security, availability, and audit obligations. Purpose: Replace the outdated service platform and appointment workflow with an accessible, auditable capability that operations can sustain.

    Measurable objectives and success criteria: Deliver an accessible appointment portal, configured cloud service platform, validated migration of active customer records, and operational training and transition. Success requires verified WCAG 2.2 AA accessibility, self-service appointment scheduling, validated migration evidence, 99.9 percent production availability, seven-year retention and auditable deletion, formal acceptance of applicable deliverables, and an operational handoff capable of supporting the service. The benefit owner will separately measure the planned service-time improvement after transition; that benefit is not deemed realized merely because the project closes.

    High-level scope: Configure and integrate the cloud service platform; create the accessible appointment portal; migrate and validate active customer records; prepare operations, training, support, and transition evidence. Exclusions: Long-term service operation after the agreed transition, unrelated enterprise analytics, and replacement of systems outside the approved customer-service boundary.

    Major deliverables: Cloud service platform; accessible appointment portal; migrated customer records; operations training and transition package. Summary milestones: Start authorized for 2026-09-15; target service transition 2027-11-19; target project close 2027-12-17, subject to detailed planning and authorized change.

    Summary financial authorization: Up to USD 2,400,000 for the authorized project boundary, with detailed estimating, time phasing, contingency treatment, and any governance-held reserve decisions to be developed and approved through planning. This amount is an authorization boundary, not an assertion that the detailed cost baseline already exists.

    High-level risks: Migration-quality defects may delay acceptance; seller capacity may delay integration; accessibility or retention evidence may be insufficient at a release gate; operational adoption may be slower than expected. Assumptions: Required seller capacity and test environments will be available when planned; active records can be mapped with acceptable quality; operational representatives can participate in design and training. Constraints: Accessibility and retention obligations, approved financial authority, target transition window, contractual terms, specialist availability, and required acceptance evidence.

    Key stakeholders: Sponsor, customer-service director, compliance authority, support-team representative, external implementation seller, frontline user representative, operations owner, benefit owner, customer or acceptance authority, functional managers, and delivery participants. The Stakeholder Register will expand and maintain this information using controlled identifiers where appropriate.

    Project-manager authority: Coordinate detailed planning and authorized delivery; request and apply resources made available under the governing assignments; convene contributors; maintain integrated project information; make decisions within approved thresholds; and escalate funding, contract, compliance, acceptance, resource, or strategic matters to the proper authority. The project manager may not unilaterally change contracts, waive obligations, accept deliverables for the customer, or exceed the financial authorization.

    Approval requirements: Sponsor approval of the charter; procurement-authority approval of contractual actions; compliance and quality decisions within their mandates; customer acceptance against agreed criteria; operations-owner readiness for transition; and governance approval for changes beyond delegated thresholds. Exit criteria: Approved completion and transition; sponsor or governance cancellation because value is no longer justified; suspension pending unresolved compliance, feasibility, funding, or seller conditions; or reauthorization when fundamental purpose or boundaries change.

    Sponsor authorization: The Chief Customer Officer authorizes ServiceBridge Modernization within these high-level boundaries, recognizes the Service Modernization Program Manager’s delegated authority, commits sponsor support and escalation, and directs the team to develop the detailed Project Management Plan and supporting project documents.

    Why selected elements matter

    The example separates approval from detail. The USD 2.4 million amount permits an investment decision but does not pretend that work-package estimates or a cost baseline already exist. The milestone dates express external expectations without substituting for a network schedule. Risks and assumptions are visible because authorization must acknowledge uncertainty, while detailed analysis remains later work. Authority is stated with limits so that the project manager can integrate work without absorbing sponsor, functional, procurement, compliance, or acceptance decisions.

    Failure modes

    A vague charter creates multiple projects under one name: stakeholders agree to “modernize service” while imagining different outcomes, boundaries, and measures. A charter copied from a template without negotiation can appear complete while leaving authority, funding, resource access, and exit decisions unresolved.

    An over-detailed charter creates a different failure. It embeds untested requirements and schedules before investigation, making discovery look like unauthorized deviation. A charter that is never formally approved leaves the project manager dependent on personal influence and exposes functional teams and suppliers to conflicting instructions. A charter treated as permanently immutable can also conceal a fundamental change that should have triggered sponsor review or reauthorization.

    Without a charter, unclear authority and unsupported resource commitments can persist until a conflict or loss forces escalation. The remedy is not more fields. It is a decision-quality record: traceable justification, explicit boundaries, honest uncertainty, usable authority, named decision-makers, and approval evidence understood by the people expected to act on it.

    Exam distinctions

    1. The Business Case justifies investment; the charter authorizes the project or phase.
    2. The charter is high-level; the Project Management Plan explains how authorized work will be managed.
    3. High-level scope is not the detailed project scope statement, WBS, or scope baseline.
    4. Summary budget is not automatically the cost baseline, and summary milestones are not the detailed schedule baseline.
    5. Charter risks and assumptions are not substitutes for the Risk Register and Assumption Log.
    6. Key stakeholders in the charter are not the complete Stakeholder Register.
    7. The sponsor or other designated initiating authority authorizes; the project manager may contribute but does not normally self-authorize.
    8. A contract establishes obligations between parties but does not automatically resolve internal project authority.
    9. Adaptive delivery changes the level and timing of detail; it does not eliminate authorization or governance.

    These distinctions preserve the decision sequence: the Business Case justifies investment, the charter authorizes the project, and the Project Management Plan explains how the authorized work will be managed. High-level scope, summary funding, major uncertainty, and key stakeholders are therefore authorization evidence—not substitutes for the detailed baselines, registers, and plans developed later.

    Purpose

    The Project Charter records the authorization to begin the project or phase and connects the business decision to an accountable project effort.

    Why It Exists

    Without explicit authorization, planning and delivery can begin without a recognized mandate, sponsor commitment, or agreed high-level boundary.

    Business evidence informs the charter; approval changes the effort from proposed to authorized. The charter then constrains integrated planning and remains part of the closure record.

    Relationships

    The charter is not the business case and is not the detailed Project Management Plan. It authorizes; the plan explains how the authorized work will be managed.

    Common Exam Confusions

    • Treating informal agreement as project authorization.
    • Expanding the charter into a complete management plan.
    • Assuming the project manager alone authorizes the project.

    Practical Example

    A sponsor approves a charter for a customer-portal project, names the project manager, records the expected service outcome, and establishes the initial budget boundary. Detailed scope and delivery planning follow.

    Example

    Approach Atlas example focus
    Predictive / Waterfall The project charter supports an approved end-to-end plan, stage-gate review, and formal control of affected commitments.
    Adaptive / Agile The project charter is maintained close to the work, refined from feedback, and reviewed at iteration or release cadence without removing governance.
    Hybrid The project charter connects baselined infrastructure and procurement commitments with incrementally refined software delivery.

    Approach comparison

    Approach Typical timing and detail Ownership and approval Update cadence
    Predictive / Waterfall Planned earlier and detailed to the level needed for the approved end-to-end commitment. Central approval is common when a baseline, contract, or gate is affected. At control dates, reporting periods, and approved changes.
    Adaptive / Agile Refined near the work and release horizon; planning and documentation still occur. Teams and product roles decide within delegated boundaries; specialist and governance authority remains. Continuously, per iteration, or per release according to need.
    Hybrid Fixed obligations are detailed early while solution detail is progressively elaborated. Distributed delivery decisions connect to centralized decisions for baselines, contracts, compliance, and acceptance. Iteration-level updates plus integrated governance reviews.

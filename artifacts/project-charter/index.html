    Project Charter

    A project charter is the document that officially authorizes a project.

    Before the charter is approved, the work may be an idea, proposal, request, or possible investment. Once the sponsor or another authorized leader approves the charter, the organization is formally agreeing that the project should begin.

    The charter also gives the project manager authority to begin organizing the work and to use organizational resources within the limits established by the organization.

    What is a project?

    A project is temporary work undertaken to create a particular product, service, result, capability, or change.

    Temporary means the project has a beginning and an end. It does not mean the project must be short. A project can last weeks or years, and the result may continue long after the project ends. Project work is different from routine operations, which repeat or continue without a planned ending.

    Organizations undertake projects because they expect a useful result, benefit, obligation, improvement, or change. Because a project uses time, money, people, and other resources, someone with authority must decide that it should exist. The charter records that decision.

    What does the charter do?

    Approving the charter means that the organization has agreed, at a high level:

    • why the project is being undertaken;
    • what it is expected to accomplish;
    • what broad work is included;
    • what major limits or conditions apply;
    • who will sponsor the project;
    • who will lead it;
    • what authority the project manager has;
    • whether detailed planning may begin.

    The charter gives the team enough direction to begin planning. It does not contain the complete project plan. Detailed requirements, estimates, schedules, responsibilities, and control methods are developed later.

    Without a charter, the project manager may be asked to lead work without clear authority. Teams may disagree about what was approved, and resources may be committed to an effort the organization has never formally authorized.

    What is included in a project charter?

    Business need and project purpose

    The business need describes why the project is being considered. It may be:

    • a problem to solve;
    • an opportunity;
    • a legal or regulatory requirement;
    • a customer need;
    • an operational improvement;
    • a strategic objective.

    The project purpose states the result or change the project is intended to produce. The organization should not authorize a project unless it knows why the project is needed.

    Measurable objectives

    Objectives describe what the project is expected to achieve. They should be specific enough that the organization can later determine whether the project accomplished what it was authorized to do.

    For example: “Reduce average customer-service response time from 48 hours to 12 hours within six months of launch.” This is more useful than a vague objective such as “improve customer service.”

    Success criteria

    Success criteria describe how the sponsor and stakeholders will judge whether the project succeeded. Examples may include:

    • a required capability works;
    • a deadline is met;
    • a regulatory requirement is satisfied;
    • users can complete a defined task;
    • an agreed performance target is reached.

    Project completion and benefit realization may not occur at the same time. A project may deliver and transition a new service before the organization can measure its longer-term benefits.

    High-level scope

    Scope describes the broad work and results included in the project. It answers:

    • What is this project broadly responsible for?
    • What is outside the project?
    • What major result is being authorized?

    For a customer-service system project, the charter may include selecting or configuring the new system, migrating customer data, integrating the system with existing tools, training users, and launching the service. It may exclude replacing the billing system, redesigning unrelated websites, and restructuring the entire customer-service department.

    Scope is included so the sponsor and team share an initial boundary for the work. Detailed requirements and detailed scope are developed later during planning. The charter sets the initial boundary without pretending that all details are already known.

    Preliminary requirements

    Preliminary requirements are the major needs already known when the project is authorized. For example:

    • the system must meet accessibility requirements;
    • customer records must be retained for a defined period;
    • the service must support a stated number of users;
    • the solution must integrate with an existing platform.

    These are not the complete requirements documentation. They record the important needs that affect the decision to authorize the project.

    Major deliverables

    Deliverables are the main products, services, or results the project is expected to produce. Examples include a configured service platform, migrated data, training materials, tested integrations, and a transition package.

    Major deliverables belong in the charter because they help the sponsor and project manager agree on the main results being authorized.

    Key stakeholders

    Stakeholders are people or groups who can affect the project, are affected by it, provide resources, make decisions, use or accept the result, or may support or resist the work.

    The charter identifies the most important stakeholders known at the beginning. These may include the sponsor, customer, major departments, users, regulators, vendors, and project manager.

    Stakeholders are included because their authority, needs, expectations, and involvement may affect whether the project can succeed. The charter does not replace the Stakeholder Register, which records stakeholder information in greater detail.

    Approved budget or financial boundary

    The charter may include an approved budget, funding limit, expected cost range, or another financial boundary. The organization needs to know how much money it is willing to commit before detailed planning begins.

    The charter’s budget is a high-level authorization or boundary. It is not necessarily the final cost baseline. Detailed cost estimates and the cost baseline are developed later.

    For ServiceBridge, the charter may state that the project is authorized within a USD 2.4 million financial boundary. Detailed cost planning later determines how that amount is allocated.

    Summary schedule and milestones

    The charter includes major dates or milestones that matter to the authorization decision. Examples include a regulatory deadline, planned launch, contract date, phase completion, or required transition date.

    Milestones are included because a project may not be feasible if a major date cannot be met. The charter does not contain the complete project schedule.

    High-level risks

    The charter identifies major uncertainties that could affect the decision to proceed. For example, critical technology may not work as expected, required data may be incomplete, a key resource may be unavailable, an approval may be delayed, or a vendor may miss the required timeline.

    Risks are included so the sponsor understands the most important uncertainty before authorizing the project. The charter is not the full Risk Register. Detailed risk identification, analysis, ownership, and response planning occur later.

    Assumptions

    An assumption is something treated as true for planning even though it has not yet been fully confirmed. The team may assume that a subject-matter expert will be available, existing data is usable, a vendor can meet a stated date, or required funding will be released.

    Assumptions are included because a false assumption may require a different schedule, budget, scope, or decision. Initial assumptions and constraints are also recorded in the Assumption Log, where they can be checked and monitored.

    Constraints and boundaries

    Constraints are limits the project must work within. Examples include a fixed deadline, legal requirement, approved budget, required technology, contract condition, or limited staffing. Boundaries clarify where the project begins and ends.

    Constraints and boundaries belong in the charter because they prevent planning from assuming options the organization does not actually have.

    Sponsor

    The sponsor is the person or authority that supports the project at the organizational level and normally authorizes or secures authorization for it.

    Depending on the organization, the sponsor may support the business need, obtain funding, resolve major organizational obstacles, approve the charter, and support the project manager. Not every sponsor performs every task in every organization.

    Project manager and authority

    The charter identifies the project manager or project lead and states the authority granted to that person. The authority may include organizing project planning, coordinating approved resources, convening stakeholders, making decisions within stated limits, and escalating decisions outside those limits.

    The project manager is named because that person cannot be held responsible for organizing the project without enough authority to do the work. The charter does not give universal authority over funding, contracts, personnel, or governance. Those limits depend on the organization.

    Approval requirements

    Approval requirements identify important decisions or results that require formal approval. Examples include charter approval, funding approval, design approval, regulatory approval, and final acceptance.

    The charter identifies the important approval points. Detailed approval procedures may be developed later.

    Exit criteria

    Exit criteria describe conditions under which the project or a phase may be completed, stopped, suspended, or reconsidered. Examples include:

    • required funding is not obtained;
    • the solution is no longer feasible;
    • a legal requirement cannot be met;
    • the business need disappears;
    • required acceptance is achieved.

    Exit criteria belong in the charter because the organization should know not only why it is starting the project, but also what conditions may justify ending or stopping it.

    What is not in the charter?

    The charter is high-level. It does not replace:

    • detailed requirements documentation;
    • the detailed Project Scope Statement;
    • the Work Breakdown Structure;
    • the schedule baseline;
    • the cost baseline;
    • the full Risk Register;
    • the detailed Stakeholder Register;
    • the Communications Management Plan;
    • the Procurement Management Plan;
    • the complete Project Management Plan;
    • a Sprint Backlog or detailed Product Backlog.

    The charter approves the project and defines its initial boundaries. Planning later determines exactly how the work will be performed, scheduled, budgeted, monitored, and controlled.

    How is the charter created?

    1. A business need, opportunity, obligation, or problem is identified.
    2. The Business Case and benefits information are reviewed.
    3. Agreements, major constraints, and known organizational conditions are considered.
    4. The sponsor, proposed project manager, and key contributors clarify the project’s purpose, objectives, boundaries, major requirements, budget, milestones, risks, assumptions, and authority.
    5. Important disagreements or feasibility problems are resolved or escalated.
    6. The sponsor or authorized governance body approves the charter.
    7. Detailed planning begins.

    The exact procedure depends on the organization. A project manager may help prepare the charter, but the sponsor or another authorized leader issues or approves it.

    ServiceBridge example

    A company has decided that its current customer-service system is too slow, difficult to audit, and inaccessible to some users.

    The proposed ServiceBridge project would:

    • configure a replacement service platform;
    • migrate existing customer records;
    • connect the new platform to required systems;
    • train employees;
    • transition the service into operations.

    The draft charter identifies:

    • Business need: improve response time, accessibility, and auditability.
    • Objective: reduce average response time and provide an accessible service before the required transition date.
    • High-level scope: platform configuration, migration, integrations, testing, training, and transition.
    • Exclusions: replacement of unrelated billing systems.
    • Budget: authorization within a USD 2.4 million financial boundary.
    • Milestones: configuration, migration testing, user testing, launch, and operational transition.
    • Stakeholders: sponsor, customer-service leadership, users, compliance staff, technology teams, seller, and operations.
    • Risks: migration quality, seller capacity, integration difficulty, and compliance delays.
    • Assumptions: required data can be migrated and key specialists will be available.
    • Project manager authority: coordinate planning and approved resources, while funding increases, contract changes, and compliance acceptance remain with the appropriate authorities.

    Once the sponsor approves the charter, the project manager may begin detailed planning. The team still needs to develop the full requirements, scope baseline, schedule, budget, Risk Register, and other plans.

    Charter compared with related documents

    The documents below support different decisions. The Business Case explains the investment, the charter authorizes the project, and planning documents develop the detail.

    Document Main purpose
    Business Case Explains why the organization should invest in the project
    Project Charter Formally authorizes the project and establishes its high-level direction and authority
    Assumption Log Records assumptions and constraints that must be checked or monitored
    Stakeholder Register Records detailed information about stakeholders
    Project Management Plan Explains how the authorized project will be planned, executed, monitored, controlled, and closed
    Project Scope Statement Defines the detailed project and product scope developed during planning
    Scope Baseline Contains the approved scope statement, WBS, and WBS dictionary used for scope control

    Predictive, adaptive, and hybrid use

    Predictive

    The charter authorizes the project before detailed requirements, scope, schedule, and cost baselines are developed.

    Adaptive

    An adaptive project still needs purpose, authorization, funding, stakeholders, authority, and boundaries. The charter may be shorter and may describe the product vision, desired outcomes, major constraints, funding boundaries, key stakeholders, and who may make which decisions.

    Detailed scope may be developed through a Product Backlog rather than fixed completely at the start. Scrum does not require a Project Charter, although the organization may still require one to authorize and fund the work.

    Hybrid

    A hybrid project may use the charter to establish funding, governance, contracts, compliance obligations, major milestones, high-level scope, and project authority. Adaptive teams may then develop and deliver detailed product work iteratively.

    Common exam distinctions

    These distinctions separate the charter’s authorization purpose from the detailed plans, baselines, registers, and team-level work records developed after approval.

    • The Project Charter formally authorizes the project.
    • The sponsor or another authorized leader approves or issues it; the project manager does not normally self-authorize the project.
    • The charter gives the project manager authority to apply organizational resources within defined limits.
    • The charter contains high-level scope, requirements, budget, milestones, risks, assumptions, stakeholders, and authority.
    • The charter is not the Project Management Plan.
    • The charter budget is not necessarily the final cost baseline.
    • High-level risks in the charter are not the full Risk Register.
    • Initial stakeholders in the charter do not replace the Stakeholder Register.
    • The charter is created before detailed planning.
    • The Assumption Log is also produced or initiated during Develop Project Charter.

    Delivery approach applicability

    →Predictive↻Adaptive⇄Hybrid
    Predictive treatment
    The predictive variant uses greater upfront definition, formal approval, baselining, or controlled updates where appropriate.
    Adaptive equivalent or treatment
    The adaptive variant preserves the management purpose while using iterative, incremental, backlog-based, or team-owned treatment where appropriate.
    Hybrid treatment
    The hybrid variant connects adaptive working detail with predictive governance, funding, compliance, contract, milestone, or approval needs.
    Why these badges
    The canonical artifact resource provides distinct predictive, adaptive, and hybrid examples, so the badges derive from those same variants.

    Purpose

    The Project Charter records the authorization to begin the project or phase and connects the business decision to an accountable project effort.

    Why It Exists

    Without explicit authorization, planning and delivery can begin without a recognized mandate, sponsor commitment, or agreed high-level boundary.

    Contains

    3. Sponsor and project-manager authority.
    4. Summary milestone and funding information where established.
    5. Approval evidence.

    Lifecycle

    Business evidence informs the charter; approval changes the effort from proposed to authorized. The charter then constrains integrated planning and remains part of the closure record.

    Relationships

    The charter is not the business case and is not the detailed Project Management Plan. It authorizes; the plan explains how the authorized work will be managed.

    Common Exam Confusions

    • Treating informal agreement as project authorization.
    • Expanding the charter into a complete management plan.
    • Assuming the project manager alone authorizes the project.

    Practical Example

    A sponsor approves a charter for a customer-portal project, names the project manager, records the expected service outcome, and establishes the initial budget boundary. Detailed scope and delivery planning follow.

    Example

    Approach Atlas example focus
    Predictive / Waterfall The project charter supports an approved end-to-end plan, stage-gate review, and formal control of affected commitments.
    Adaptive / Agile The project charter is maintained close to the work, refined from feedback, and reviewed at iteration or release cadence without removing governance.
    Hybrid The project charter connects baselined infrastructure and procurement commitments with incrementally refined software delivery.

    Approach comparison

    Approach Typical timing and detail Ownership and approval Update cadence
    Predictive / Waterfall Planned earlier and detailed to the level needed for the approved end-to-end commitment. Central approval is common when a baseline, contract, or gate is affected. At control dates, reporting periods, and approved changes.
    Adaptive / Agile Refined near the work and release horizon; planning and documentation still occur. Teams and product roles decide within delegated boundaries; specialist and governance authority remains. Continuously, per iteration, or per release according to need.
    Hybrid Fixed obligations are detailed early while solution detail is progressively elaborated. Distributed delivery decisions connect to centralized decisions for baselines, contracts, compliance, and acceptance. Iteration-level updates plus integrated governance reviews.

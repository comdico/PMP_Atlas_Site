    How engagement gaps support decisions

    The Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix makes a different problem visible from stakeholder classification. It compares the stakeholder’s current engagement toward the project or change with the engagement state needed for project success. The gap helps the team decide whether to build awareness, understand resistance, invite participation, sustain support, or strengthen active leadership.

    The state labels describe engagement, not rank, power, authority, or personality. A powerful regulator can be Neutral, a low-power user can be Supportive, and a sponsor can be Supportive without yet providing the Leading advocacy needed to remove organizational barriers. The matrix therefore complements rather than replaces the Power–Interest Grid.

    Evidence and reassessment

    Current-state assessment should come from observable evidence: participation, decisions, feedback, advocacy, questions, objections, follow-through, demonstrations, interviews, surveys, or direct collaboration. Desired state follows from project needs rather than a wish that every stakeholder become Leading. Some stakeholders only need awareness or neutral cooperation; forcing a higher desired state can waste attention or damage trust.

    The team records the evidence, gap, action, owner, and review date, then reassesses after engagement activity or material context change. Sensitive judgments should use stable identifiers, professional language, and access controls. A changed attitude is not automatically a formal change request, though its consequences may create a risk, issue, or controlled change decision.

    The desired state should be tied to a needed behavior. “Supportive” might mean timely participation in validation, while “Leading” might mean visible advocacy and removal of cross-functional barriers. Without that behavioral definition, reviewers can agree on a label while expecting different actions. A review date makes the assessment testable: the team can examine whether the chosen engagement action produced observable change.

    Causes matter more than labels. Resistance can arise from credible workload, safety, privacy, job-impact, feasibility, or trust concerns. The response should investigate and address those causes within the stakeholder’s context. Persuasion is not automatically the right action; changing scope, supplying evidence, resolving an issue, escalating an authority gap, or accepting a neutral position may be more responsible.

    Reassessment occurs at a useful cadence and after triggers such as a governance decision, demonstration, rejected deliverable, organizational change, new risk, or transition event. The owner records evidence rather than merely changing C and D markers. Where the desired state is not achieved, the team decides whether to revise the action, change the desired state, escalate, or alter the project decision that depended on engagement.

    Matrix ownership does not create authority over the stakeholder. The project manager or engagement lead may maintain the record, while relationship owners and decision authorities contribute evidence and approve actions within their remit. Privacy, labor, legal, or cultural constraints may limit what can be recorded or shared, so professional judgment and access control remain part of the method.

    ServiceBridge interpretation

    ServiceBridge records STK-01, the sponsor, as Supportive with a desired state of Leading. The evidence shows approval and barrier removal but limited advocacy. The selected action is not “send more status”: the sponsor is asked to lead a governance briefing and reinforce the service outcome with functional leaders.

    STK-04, the support-team representative, is Resistant because of credible workload, training, and support-procedure concerns. The action is a workflow walkthrough and co-design of training and escalation, not an attempt to suppress resistance. STK-02 is already Leading, so the strategy sustains decision access rather than manufacturing a gap.

    How the stakeholder records fit together

    The Stakeholder Register answers who is involved and what is known. The Power–Interest Grid helps prioritize attention. This matrix asks what engagement exists and what is needed. The Stakeholder Engagement Plan defines strategies and actions, and the Communications Management Plan defines information delivery. Each record should link to the others without becoming a duplicate master spreadsheet.

    Failure modes

    The matrix fails when states are assigned from rumor, personal liking, or a single meeting; when every desired state is Leading; or when sensitive assessments circulate as a public ranking. It also fails when the team updates labels without changing an engagement strategy or recording evidence.

    Consequences include damaged trust, ineffective actions, overlooked causes of resistance, and false confidence. Useful assessment separates observation from interpretation, chooses a proportionate desired state, assigns action and review responsibility, and treats resistance as information rather than misconduct.

    Exam distinctions

    Engagement state is not power, authority, interest, or formal project role. Resistant does not mean low power; Leading does not grant project-management authority. The matrix supports Plan, Manage, and Monitor Stakeholder Engagement but is not the formal Stakeholder Engagement Plan or the Communications Management Plan.

    Sources and synthesis status

    Lesson 3 directly supports the five current/desired states, C and D markers, continuing engagement, feedback, and reassessment. The ServiceBridge interpretation, failure modes, privacy application, and record-family explanation are Atlas synthesis. The Atlas does not prescribe a universal approval authority, numeric scale, or mandatory matrix format.

    Definition

    Compares each stakeholder's current engagement state with the desired state needed for project success so engagement gaps can inform strategy and monitoring.

    It is an assessment and data-representation tool that may be maintained as a working project artifact. It is not the formal output of Plan Stakeholder Engagement; that output remains the Stakeholder Engagement Plan.

    Purpose

    The matrix makes the difference between actual stakeholder engagement and the engagement needed for project success visible. The gap informs engagement strategy, actions, communication choices, ownership, and review cadence; the matrix does not contain the complete strategy.

    Sensitive stakeholder assessment

    Stakeholder-engagement assessments may contain sensitive judgments. Use stakeholder numbers or identifiers rather than names where appropriate; restrict access when necessary; use professional, factual language; avoid insulting or speculative descriptions; separate identity data from sensitive assessments when required; and follow organizational storage and access policy.

    Where it fits in the lifecycle

    Identify Stakeholders → Stakeholder Register → stakeholder analysis → current and desired engagement assessment → Plan Stakeholder Engagement and the Stakeholder Engagement Plan → engagement activities → monitoring of actual versus desired engagement → appropriate plan, communication, issue, risk, lesson, or change updates.

    A change in attitude does not automatically require integrated change control. Formal change control applies only when a proposed response crosses a controlled commitment or decision threshold.

    Related processes

    Plan Stakeholder Engagement

    The matrix helps identify gaps between current and desired engagement and informs strategy; the formal process output remains the Stakeholder Engagement Plan.

    Manage Stakeholder Engagement

    Engagement actions address needs, resistance, support, and involvement; observed changes may update the working assessment.

    Monitor Stakeholder Engagement

    Actual engagement is reassessed against desired engagement and may lead to matrix, register, plan, communication, issue, risk, lesson, or change updates without making every attitude change a formal change request.

    Communication planning, management, and monitoring support the engagement strategy through appropriate information and feedback. See Plan Communications Management, Manage Communications, Monitor Communications.

    Related Decision Guides: A Risk Occurs or an Impediment Exceeds Team Authority and A Stakeholder Requests a Baseline Change. These guides apply only when the observed engagement condition creates the corresponding issue, impediment, risk, or controlled-change decision.

    Inputs

    1. The Stakeholder Register and stakeholder classifications.
    2. The project management plan, including stakeholder and communications planning information.
    3. Observations and feedback from engagement activities, reviews, demonstrations, meetings, interviews, surveys, and direct collaboration.
    4. Issue, risk, lesson, change, governance, and organizational-context information relevant to engagement.
    5. Expert judgment, emotional intelligence, and interpersonal skills applied to factual evidence.

    Typical structure

    1. Stakeholder identifier rather than a personal name where sensitivity requires it.
    2. Project role or group.
    3. Current engagement state and desired engagement state.
    4. Evidence supporting the assessment.
    5. The engagement gap and recommended action.
    6. Action owner, review date, and next review date.
    7. Change history and access classification where required.

    Current and desired engagement states

    C marks the current assessed engagement state. D marks the desired state needed for project success. C/D may occupy one state when no change is required. These states describe engagement toward the project or change, not formal authority. A resistant stakeholder may still have high power or influence, and Leading does not necessarily mean project leadership or management authority. Organizations may tailor the labels.

    Unaware

    The stakeholder does not know about the project or its potential effects.

    Resistant

    The stakeholder knows about the project but opposes it, its effects, or the proposed change.

    Neutral

    The stakeholder is aware but is neither actively supportive nor actively resistant.

    Supportive

    The stakeholder supports the project or change and may assist its success.

    Leading

    The stakeholder is actively engaged in helping the project succeed and influencing others positively.

    How to assess the engagement gap

    Compare C with D, document observable evidence, identify why the gap matters, and select a proportionate engagement action. Do not assign a numeric score unless the organization deliberately adopts and documents an additional method.

    Stakeholder ID Unaware Resistant Neutral Supportive Leading
    STK-01 C D
    STK-02 C,D
    STK-03 C D
    1. STK-01: current resistance must move toward support; the engagement strategy should address causes, not merely send more status messages.
    2. STK-02: current and desired are both Neutral, so no state movement is required, although monitoring continues.
    3. STK-03: the largest gap runs from Neutral to Leading and needs deliberate involvement and influence-building.

    How the matrix is updated

    Update the assessment regularly and when evidence changes—for example at stakeholder reviews, governance reviews, phase boundaries, major change points, iteration reviews, demonstrations, or material issue and risk events. Record the evidence, date, owner, and next review. Do not change a state solely from rumor or personal dislike.

    Roles and responsibilities

    Project manager or stakeholder-engagement lead

    Facilitates assessment, coordinates strategies, monitors changes, protects sensitive information, and escalates matters outside delegated authority.

    Project team

    Provides factual observations, identifies emerging needs or resistance, and supports engagement actions.

    Sponsor

    May engage influential or resistant stakeholders and resolve strategic barriers but does not automatically maintain the matrix.

    Product Owner or product leadership

    May assess customer, user, and business engagement in adaptive or product-oriented delivery but does not automatically own all project stakeholder engagement.

    Scrum Team

    Gathers frequent feedback through collaboration and review and may use lighter-weight assessment; Scrum does not prescribe this matrix.

    Governance or functional leaders

    May support engagement where authority, resources, or organizational change are involved. No universal approval authority is assigned to the matrix.

    Predictive, adaptive, and hybrid treatment

    Delivery approach applicability

    →Predictive↻Adaptive⇄Hybrid
    Predictive treatment
    The predictive variant uses greater upfront definition, formal approval, baselining, or controlled updates where appropriate.
    Adaptive equivalent or treatment
    The adaptive variant preserves the management purpose while using iterative, incremental, backlog-based, or team-owned treatment where appropriate.
    Hybrid treatment
    The hybrid variant connects adaptive working detail with predictive governance, funding, compliance, contract, milestone, or approval needs.
    Why these badges
    The canonical artifact resource provides distinct predictive, adaptive, and hybrid examples, so the badges derive from those same variants.

    Additional treatment context

    Predictive projects may maintain a formal controlled matrix; adaptive teams may use lighter and more frequent assessment; hybrid work can combine formal governance-stakeholder assessment with rapid customer and user feedback.

    Predictive: A formal, access-controlled matrix may be reviewed at planning updates, governance reviews, phase boundaries, major change points, and scheduled stakeholder reviews.

    Adaptive: The purpose remains useful, but the representation may be lightweight and updated more frequently from iteration reviews, customer feedback, demonstrations, direct collaboration, retrospective observations, and backlog participation. A formal matrix is not universally required.

    Hybrid: Formal assessment may cover sponsors, regulators, sellers, governance stakeholders, and organizational leaders while customers, users, product stakeholders, and delivery participants are assessed through a lighter and faster cadence.

    Relationship to other stakeholder artifacts

    Tool or artifact Primary question
    Stakeholder Register Who are the stakeholders and what are their roles, interests, influence, requirements, and classifications?
    Power/Interest or other assessment grid How should stakeholders be classified according to power, interest, influence, impact, urgency, or legitimacy?
    Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix What is each stakeholder’s current engagement state and what engagement state is desired?
    Stakeholder Engagement Plan What strategies and actions will be used to engage stakeholders? See Plan Stakeholder Engagement.
    Communications Management Plan What information will be communicated, by whom, how, when, and to whom? See Plan Communications Management.

    These records work together but are not interchangeable.

    1. PMI Process Groups: A Practice Guide — official predictive process context; it is not the source of the Atlas matrix example.

    Exam distinctions

    1. The Stakeholder Register identifies and classifies stakeholders.
    2. The engagement assessment matrix compares current and desired engagement.
    3. The Stakeholder Engagement Plan defines strategies and actions.
    4. The Communications Management Plan defines information needs and delivery.
    5. Power/interest and salience models classify stakeholders; they do not replace engagement-state assessment.
    6. A resistant stakeholder is not necessarily low power.
    7. Leading means actively helping project success, not formal project authority.
    8. Update the matrix as engagement changes.
    9. Use stakeholder identifiers rather than sensitive names where appropriate.
    10. In adaptive work, the assessment purpose remains relevant even when the format is lightweight.

    Practical example and reusable resources

    The examples use the fictional ServiceBridge Modernization project so related artifacts share dates, budget, requirements, risks, issues, changes, deliverables, acceptance, transition, and benefits context.

    Example

    Approach Atlas example focus
    Predictive / Waterfall The stakeholder engagement assessment matrix supports an approved end-to-end plan, stage-gate review, and formal control of affected commitments.
    Adaptive / Agile The stakeholder engagement assessment matrix is maintained close to the work, refined from feedback, and reviewed at iteration or release cadence without removing governance.
    Hybrid The stakeholder engagement assessment matrix connects baselined infrastructure and procurement commitments with incrementally refined software delivery.

    Approach comparison

    Approach Typical timing and detail Ownership and approval Update cadence
    Predictive / Waterfall Planned earlier and detailed to the level needed for the approved end-to-end commitment. Central approval is common when a baseline, contract, or gate is affected. At control dates, reporting periods, and approved changes.
    Adaptive / Agile Refined near the work and release horizon; planning and documentation still occur. Teams and product roles decide within delegated boundaries; specialist and governance authority remains. Continuously, per iteration, or per release according to need.
    Hybrid Fixed obligations are detailed early while solution detail is progressively elaborated. Distributed delivery decisions connect to centralized decisions for baselines, contracts, compliance, and acceptance. Iteration-level updates plus integrated governance reviews.

    Stakeholder Power–Interest Grid

    How classification supports decisions

    The Power–Interest Grid is useful because stakeholder attention cannot be allocated from visibility or job title alone. It asks two bounded questions: how much power does the stakeholder have to affect project decisions or outcomes, and how much interest do they have in the project and its effects? Evidence-based placement helps the team decide where direct involvement, decision access, information, feedback, and monitoring deserve emphasis.

    The quadrant labels are management prompts, not automatic prescriptions. Manage closely signals that both decision influence and interest are high, but it does not mean constant meetings. Keep satisfied calls for decision-relevant engagement with a high-power, lower-interest stakeholder. Keep informed recognizes that high-interest affected people deserve useful information and feedback even with limited formal power. Monitor means proportionate attention and reassessment, not neglect.

    Evidence and reassessment

    Power evidence can include funding control, resource authority, approval or acceptance rights, legal or contractual authority, specialist decision rights, and the ability to accelerate or constrain outcomes. Interest evidence can include degree of impact, participation, information demand, concern, adoption responsibility, or attention to outcomes. Job title alone is insufficient, and a stakeholder can be highly powerful while resistant, unaware, or only intermittently involved.

    Placement should be documented with a rationale and revisited when authority, impact, lifecycle stage, behavior, project conditions, or organizational structure changes. Numeric scoring can structure discussion, but the included scoring approach is optional Atlas synthesis rather than a PMI-prescribed calculation. Scores do not remove judgment or create permanent truth.

    Evidence quality matters because power is contextual. A functional manager may control a specialist during planning but have little authority over customer acceptance. A regulator may rarely participate yet retain decisive release power. A community representative may lack formal authority while influencing legitimacy, adoption, or political support. The assessment should name the relevant decision or effect instead of assigning a vague global score.

    Interest is also specific to the project’s current state. Someone may care intensely about a design decision and then need only exception reporting during implementation. Another stakeholder may show little early interest but become central during transition. Interviews, attendance patterns, decision records, escalation history, operational impact, and direct observation provide stronger evidence than assumptions based on title or personality.

    Reassessment should be triggered by material events as well as scheduled reviews: a sponsor change, new contract, changed regulation, major risk, phase transition, release, reorganization, or newly affected user group. The analyst records why a placement changed and then checks whether engagement and communication strategies still fit. Reclassification without an action decision adds maintenance work but no management value.

    Ownership should be explicit. The project manager may maintain the analysis, but contributors closest to a stakeholder supply evidence and the authority responsible for a decision confirms its governance implications. Access should be limited when disclosure could damage trust, expose personal data, or distort negotiations. The useful output is a defensible engagement decision, not a public ranking of people.

    ServiceBridge interpretation

    ServiceBridge places the sponsor in Manage closely because funding and strategic authority combine with active outcome ownership. The compliance authority is Keep satisfied because release-gate power is high while routine design interest is intentionally limited. The frontline user representative is Keep informed: formal authority is low, but direct impact and feedback value are high.

    Those placements produce different actions. The sponsor receives concise decision briefs and participates in strategic barriers. The compliance authority agrees evidence checkpoints rather than attending every workshop. The frontline representative receives accessible demonstrations and visible feedback closure. The grid informs those choices; the Stakeholder Engagement Plan and Communications Management Plan define them.

    Failure modes

    The grid fails when teams equate organizational rank with power, treat low power as low importance, classify from personality judgments, or use one placement for the entire project. It also fails when quadrant labels are copied into a plan without explaining actual stakeholder needs, authority, evidence, or communication preferences.

    Consequences include ignored affected groups, over-communication of senior stakeholders, missing approvals, privacy harm, and stale strategies. The remedy is factual evidence, professional language, restricted access where appropriate, and scheduled or event-driven reassessment.

    Exam distinctions

    The Stakeholder Register identifies and describes stakeholders. The Power–Interest Grid classifies power and interest. The engagement assessment matrix compares current and desired engagement. The engagement plan defines strategy, while communications planning defines information delivery. Power is not engagement state, interest is not approval authority, and Monitor does not mean ignore.

    Sources and synthesis status

    Lesson 3 directly supports the Power/interest axes, the four quadrant labels, identifier use, and mapping method. The content rationales, evidence examples, ServiceBridge actions, optional scoring, failure modes, and approach treatment are Atlas synthesis. The Atlas withholds any claim that numeric thresholds or one communication cadence is officially prescribed.

    Artifact type: Stakeholder analysis and data-representation tool / working artifact

    Definition and purpose

    Classifies stakeholders by evidenced power and interest so engagement and communication effort can be tailored proportionately.

    The grid supports prioritization; it does not grant authority, prescribe a complete strategy, or replace professional judgment.

    Sensitive stakeholder assessment

    Use identifiers rather than personal names where appropriate, document observable evidence, restrict sensitive judgments, and follow organizational access policy.

    Axes and evidence

    Power: Ability to influence project decisions, resources, approvals, constraints, acceptance, or outcomes.

    Interest: Degree of attention, concern, involvement, or impact associated with the project and its outcomes.

    Use observable authority, resource, approval, impact, participation, and feedback evidence. Do not infer placement from job title, visibility, personality, or personal preference alone.

    Four quadrants

    Manage closely

    High power · High interest

    Maintain active two-way engagement, timely decision access, and frequent evidence-based review.

    Keep satisfied

    High power · Low interest

    Provide decision-relevant information and consult at appropriate thresholds without unnecessary detail.

    Keep informed

    Low power · High interest

    Provide useful updates, invite feedback, and make participation routes visible.

    Monitor

    Low power · Low interest

    Watch for material changes and communicate proportionately.

    The quadrant headings and explicit axis text communicate placement without relying on color.

    Qualitative method

    1. Identify stakeholders and use identifiers rather than personal names where sensitivity requires it.
    2. Gather observable evidence for power and interest from authority, decisions, resources, impact, participation, and feedback.
    3. Assess each axis qualitatively as High or Low under documented project criteria.
    4. Place each stakeholder in one quadrant and record the rationale.
    5. Tailor communication and engagement approaches to the evidence and context.
    6. Review with appropriate contributors while protecting sensitive judgments.
    7. Reassess when authority, interest, impact, behavior, project conditions, or lifecycle stage changes.

    Optional numeric scoring — Atlas synthesis

    Optional Atlas implementation aid—not PMI-prescribed

    Numeric scoring is an optional Atlas implementation aid, not a PMI-prescribed calculation and not required to use the grid. The downloadable workbook uses four 1–5 indicators per axis and an editable example threshold of 3.0. Define indicators and thresholds before assessment, retain the evidence, and do not imply false precision.

    Power indicators: Decision authority; Resource control; Approval or acceptance authority; Ability to constrain or accelerate outcomes.

    Interest indicators: Degree affected; Information demand; Participation level; Attention to outcomes.

    Lifecycle and process use

    Identify Stakeholders establishes and initially classifies stakeholders. Planning uses the grid with the Stakeholder Register to shape stakeholder-engagement and communications strategies. Manage Stakeholder Engagement and Manage Communications apply those strategies. Monitoring reassesses the evidence and placement as power, interest, impact, or context changes.

    A placement change does not automatically require integrated change control; use formal change control only when a proposed response crosses a controlled commitment or authority threshold.

    Roles and responsibilities

    The project manager or stakeholder-engagement lead facilitates and safeguards the assessment. Team members and subject-matter contributors supply factual evidence. Sponsors, product roles, functional leaders, governance bodies, and other stakeholders contribute within their authority. No universal approval owner is implied.

    Predictive, adaptive, and hybrid treatment

    Delivery approach applicability

    →Predictive↻Adaptive⇄Hybrid
    Predictive treatment
    The predictive variant uses greater upfront definition, formal approval, baselining, or controlled updates where appropriate.
    Adaptive equivalent or treatment
    The adaptive variant preserves the management purpose while using iterative, incremental, backlog-based, or team-owned treatment where appropriate.
    Hybrid treatment
    The hybrid variant connects adaptive working detail with predictive governance, funding, compliance, contract, milestone, or approval needs.
    Why these badges
    The canonical artifact resource provides distinct predictive, adaptive, and hybrid examples, so the badges derive from those same variants.

    Additional treatment context

    Predictive projects may maintain a controlled grid; adaptive teams may reassess stakeholders lightly and frequently; hybrid work can combine both cadences.

    Predictive work may maintain a formal controlled grid. Adaptive work may use a lighter representation and reassess it frequently. Hybrid work can combine formal governance-stakeholder assessment with rapid customer and user feedback.

    Relationship to adjacent tools and artifacts

    Tool or artifact Primary question
    Stakeholder Register Who is a stakeholder and what foundational information is known?
    Power–Interest Grid How should attention be prioritized from evidenced power and interest?
    Stakeholder Engagement Assessment Matrix What is the current versus desired engagement state?
    Stakeholder Engagement Plan Which engagement strategies and actions will be used?
    Communications Management Plan / communication matrix What information goes to whom, why, when, how, and by whom?
    Potential communication channels How many unique pairwise paths are possible for n people?

    These are complementary and not interchangeable.

    Exam distinctions

    1. Power and interest classify stakeholders; they do not describe current versus desired engagement.
    2. Manage closely, Keep satisfied, Keep informed, and Monitor are quadrant labels—not guarantees of a single communication cadence.
    3. The Communications Management Plan or communication matrix defines communication requirements; the channel formula only counts potential pairwise paths.
    4. Numeric scores and the 3.0 example threshold are optional Atlas synthesis, not PMI-prescribed calculations.
    5. Reassess as stakeholders or project conditions change.

    Practical example and reusable resources

    The examples use the fictional ServiceBridge Modernization project so related artifacts share dates, budget, requirements, risks, issues, changes, deliverables, acceptance, transition, and benefits context.

    Example

    Approach Atlas example focus
    Predictive / Waterfall The stakeholder power–interest grid supports an approved end-to-end plan, stage-gate review, and formal control of affected commitments.
    Adaptive / Agile The stakeholder power–interest grid is maintained close to the work, refined from feedback, and reviewed at iteration or release cadence without removing governance.
    Hybrid The stakeholder power–interest grid connects baselined infrastructure and procurement commitments with incrementally refined software delivery.

    Approach comparison

    Approach Typical timing and detail Ownership and approval Update cadence
    Predictive / Waterfall Planned earlier and detailed to the level needed for the approved end-to-end commitment. Central approval is common when a baseline, contract, or gate is affected. At control dates, reporting periods, and approved changes.
    Adaptive / Agile Refined near the work and release horizon; planning and documentation still occur. Teams and product roles decide within delegated boundaries; specialist and governance authority remains. Continuously, per iteration, or per release according to need.
    Hybrid Fixed obligations are detailed early while solution detail is progressively elaborated. Distributed delivery decisions connect to centralized decisions for baselines, contracts, compliance, and acceptance. Iteration-level updates plus integrated governance reviews.

    Adaptive Metrics and Visual Management

    Velocity, flow measures, burn charts, cumulative flow, and visual tools for adaptive and hybrid delivery.

    Download the editable workbook See every course source mapped

    !!! note "Source boundary" The July 2026 course supports these measures and visualizations. The current Scrum Guide does not prescribe velocity or a particular chart. The May 2025 Kanban Guide requires teams using its minimal definition to track WIP, throughput, work item age, and cycle time. Atlas examples are original ServiceBridge illustrations.

    Velocity

    Velocity is the amount of estimated work fully completed by one team during one iteration, measured using that team’s chosen estimation unit, commonly story points.

    Velocity = Σ estimates of items completed to the agreed Definition of Done during the iteration

    Only fully completed items count. An unfinished item contributes zero for that iteration; 80% complete does not earn 80% of its points. Partially completed work remains unfinished and returns to or is replanned through the backlog. If work is split, remaining work may be re-estimated for future planning, but the unfinished original item never earns retroactive partial credit. Normally use the completed item’s original estimate unless the team has an explicit different policy, and calculate velocity only after the outcome is known.

    Item Estimate End-of-Sprint status Counts toward velocity
    SB-101 8 Done 8
    SB-102 5 Done 5
    SB-103 13 80% complete 0
    SB-104 3 Done 3

    Iteration velocity: 8 + 5 + 0 + 3 = 16 story points. Unfinished means zero velocity credit.

    Average velocity and a bounded forecast

    Sprint Completed points
    1 21
    2 25
    3 23
    4 27

    Average velocity = Σ completed work units ÷ number of iterations = (21 + 25 + 23 + 27) ÷ 4 = 24 story points per Sprint.

    For 100 estimated points remaining, 100 ÷ 24 = 4.17, so a whole-Sprint planning forecast rounds up to five Sprints. The recent observed velocities range from 21 to 27, producing approximately four to five Sprints (100 ÷ 27 through 100 ÷ 21, rounded up). Neither result is a guaranteed commitment. Inspect variation, team stability, changing estimates or membership, holidays and capacity, technical uncertainty, Definition of Done changes, dependencies, and scope change.

    The columns show completed estimated work, with no partial points. They can help this same team forecast. They do not prove productivity; sudden changes need context, and an upward trend may reflect changed estimating rather than better performance.

    What velocity must not be used for

    • Velocity is team-specific because story points have no standard meaning across teams.
    • Never compare team velocities or use velocity as an individual productivity measure.
    • Higher velocity does not automatically mean more value or quality; inflating estimates creates neither.
    • Management must not impose a velocity performance quota.
    • Teams receive no credit for unfinished work.
    • Velocity does not replace inspection of outcomes, quality, customer value, or flow.
    • A substantial team-membership change can make history less predictive.

    !!! tip "Exam note" When asked to calculate iteration velocity, count only work completed to the agreed Definition of Done. Story points are not hours.

    Burndown chart

    A burndown shows work remaining vertically over time or iterations horizontally. Its endpoint is zero remaining. The actual line may be compared with an ideal or guideline line, but that guideline is not a mandatory commitment. Scope additions or re-estimation can make remaining work rise; unfinished work stays in the total. Sprint burndown focuses on one Sprint, while release or product burndown covers a broader horizon. Without a visible total-scope series, burndown can obscure why the curve changed.

    At period 2, completed work rises from 16 to 28, yet remaining work rises from 84 to 87 because total scope increases from 100 to 115. The team did work; the denominator also changed.

    Burnup chart

    A burnup plots completed work increasing toward a separate total-scope line. Because the total line steps when scope changes, burnup often makes scope movement easier to understand than burndown.

    Both burn charts use exactly the same data: remaining = scope − completed for every period.

    Question Burndown Burnup
    What does the main line show? Work remaining Work completed
    Is scope change visible? Sometimes difficult to see Explicit through total-scope line
    Typical endpoint Zero remaining Completed line reaches scope line
    Common misunderstanding Flat line always means no work occurred Rising total-scope line may explain delayed completion

    Cumulative flow diagram

    A cumulative flow diagram shows cumulative work items in workflow states over time. Band thickness represents items in that state; all unfinished bands together reflect WIP. A widening band can show accumulation or a bottleneck, horizontal distance may illustrate elapsed flow time when workflow boundaries permit, and stable parallel bands generally suggest steadier flow. Context is still required.

    Ready677888910
    In Progress23344443
    Review11235653

    Visible signal: Review widens from one to six items before recovering—investigate a possible review bottleneck.

    Task board or Kanban board

    The simple path is Ready → In Progress → Review → Done. A board visualizes work state, blocked and aging items, ownership, and collaboration. A Kanban system additionally needs a Definition of Workflow with explicit start and finish points, states, policies, WIP controls, and a service level expectation. WIP control enables pull-based movement.

    Ready

    SB-120 · audit export

    SB-121 · notice copy

    In Progress WIP 2/3

    SB-116 · identity sync BLOCKED

    SB-118 · billing rules AGING 9 DAYS · MAY 19

    Review WIP 2/2

    SB-114 · intake form

    SB-115 · search filter

    Done

    SB-109 · case detail

    SB-112 · agent notes

    Not every task board is a complete Kanban system. A board alone does not establish Kanban. Scrum teams may use boards without the full Kanban method, and hybrid projects may use a board inside broader predictive governance.

    Throughput

    Throughput is the exact count of work items finished per unit of time. Throughput = completed work items ÷ time period. ServiceBridge completes 18 items in three weeks: 6 items per week. State the period, normally count items rather than points, and interpret changing item size carefully. Historical throughput can support contextual flow forecasting.

    Velocity Throughput
    Often estimate units such as story points Counts completed work items
    Common in timeboxed planning Common in flow-based forecasting
    Team-specific estimated work Actual item completion count
    Sensitive to estimation practice Sensitive to item-size variation

    Cycle time

    Cycle time is elapsed time from the defined started point until the defined finished point. It includes active work and internal waiting after the item enters the measured workflow. A request-to-delivery interval is not cycle time unless the Definition of Workflow explicitly makes the request point its started boundary.

    Lead time

    Lead time is elapsed time from a defined request, order, or commitment point until delivery. The label is incomplete unless its start and finish boundaries are named.

    Concept Start point End point Primary use
    Procurement lead time Order or requisition Item available or delivered Predictive procurement and scheduling
    Schedule lead Relative predecessor point Earlier successor start Predictive network scheduling
    Customer lead time Customer request Customer delivery End-to-end responsiveness
    Commitment lead time Work commitment or selection Delivery Delivery forecasting
    Cycle time Work started Work finished Workflow performance
    Work item age Work started Current date while unfinished Managing aging WIP

    Exact boundaries must be defined by the organization or the Kanban system’s Definition of Workflow.

    Predictive procurement lead time

    ServiceBridge orders a specialized infrastructure appliance on March 1, 2026, and it is delivered on April 15, 2026. Using elapsed calendar days—subtract the start date from the end date, excluding the start and including the end—procurement lead time is 45 days.

    The schedule models that elapsed acquisition interval or the resulting availability constraint before installation. If the plan allows too little time, the dependent installation can be delayed. This is procurement lead time, not a precedence-relationship lead. See Schedule Analysis, Risk, Compliance, and Procurement, and Plan Procurement Management.

    Predictive schedule lead

    Coding is the predecessor and Testing is the successor. Testing may begin 2 working days before Coding is fully complete. That two-day schedule lead overlaps the activities and may shorten the modeled sequence, while increasing coordination and rework risk.

    Schedule lead is a precedence-relationship adjustment. It is not customer lead time, commitment lead time, or procurement lead time. The complete network treatment remains in Schedule Analysis.

    Adaptive and Kanban boundaries

    One ServiceBridge work item has these milestones:

    Milestone Date Workflow meaning
    Request received May 1 Backlog; waiting outside WIP under this Definition of Workflow
    Committed or selected May 8 Ready; committed but still waiting outside WIP
    Work started May 10 Enters In Progress and becomes WIP
    Review started May 17 Remains WIP in Review
    Finished and delivered May 20 Reaches Done and leaves WIP

    Date-counting convention: elapsed calendar days calculated as end date minus start date; the start date is excluded and the end date is included.

    • Customer lead time = May 20 − May 1 = 19 days.
    • Commitment lead time = May 20 − May 8 = 12 days.
    • Cycle time = May 20 − May 10 = 10 days.
    • Waiting before work starts = May 10 − May 1 = 9 days; two of those days follow commitment.
    • On May 15, while the item is still unfinished, work item age = May 15 − May 10 = 5 days.
    Backlogrequest · May 1→Readycommitment · May 8→In Progressstarted · May 10 · WIP→ReviewMay 17 · WIP→Donefinished · May 20

    Before In Progress, the item is waiting but is not WIP under this Definition of Workflow. From In Progress through Review it is WIP. At Done, its completed start-to-finish duration is cycle time. While started but unfinished, start-to-current duration is work item age. WIP is the number or set of started, unfinished items; it is not itself an elapsed-time metric. Do not call time spent as WIP “lead time” unless the selected lead-time boundary is also the workflow’s started point.

    Work item age

    Work item age is elapsed time from the defined started point until the current measurement date for an unfinished item. Once the item finishes, report its cycle time rather than continuing to calculate work item age. See the Task Board or Kanban Board, Cycle Time, and the current Kanban Guide.

    !!! note "Why labels vary" Teams and tools sometimes use “lead time” and “cycle time” differently. The Kanban system’s Definition of Workflow must specify the measured start and finish points. The Atlas therefore names each boundary explicitly rather than treating a vendor tool’s default column calculation as universal.

    Control chart

    A control chart plots a process measure over time and may include a center line and control limits. Cycle time is a useful adaptive example. Points or nonrandom patterns outside expected variation may suggest special-cause variation; investigate rather than blame. Control limits describe process behavior and are not specification limits.

    The arithmetic mean is 5.625 days. The lower limit 2 and upper limit 9 are supplied illustrative inputs. The course discusses center lines, limits, and the rule of seven but does not supply an authoritative control-limit calculation, so the Atlas intentionally withholds that formula.

    Information radiator

    An information radiator is a highly visible, current display useful to the team and stakeholders—not one chart type. It can show board status, Sprint Goal, burn charts, cumulative flow, impediments, quality, release progress, or risk indicators. It should be easy to see and understand, current, and able to prompt conversation and action. A stale dashboard is not useful merely because it is visible; a current digital display can serve distributed teams.

    Impediment list and visualization

    An impediment blocks or slows work and may be tracked in an issue-management system. A useful view names description, severity, current action, owner or escalation responsibility, age, and status.

    Use the Impediment Record, Issue Log, Roles and Authority, and impediment escalation Decision Guide. Scrum Master accountability for team effectiveness does not mean personally removing every impediment. Never penalize a team for reporting blockers.

    Metrics in Scrum events

    • Sprint Planning: historical velocity can inform capacity and forecasting; it does not dictate what Developers select.
    • Daily Scrum: Developers may inspect flow, blocked work, or Sprint Goal progress; this is not a management reporting session.
    • Sprint Review: stakeholders may discuss outcomes, release forecasts, progress, and Product Backlog adaptation.
    • Sprint Retrospective: the team may inspect cycle time, bottlenecks, quality, and flow patterns for improvement.

    No Scrum event requires a particular chart. See Scrum Events and Adaptive Cadences.

    Roles, artifacts, and systems of record

    Developers or delivery-team members produce the work and maintain operational data. The Product Owner uses product and progress information for value and backlog decisions. The Scrum Master can help the team improve flow. A project manager or governance role may use aggregated forecasts in hybrid contexts, but metric ownership never grants unilateral authority and management must not turn velocity into a quota. See Roles, Responsibilities, and Authority.

    Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment, Definition of Done, a board, Impediment Record, Issue Log, Work Performance Information, Work Performance Reports, and a contextual release plan can supply or consume the data. Operational tool data remains in its designated system of record; reports are derived views, not competing records.

    Delivery-approach distinctions

    • Adaptive: velocity, burn charts, flow metrics, and boards may support iterative or flow-based delivery.
    • Scrum: velocity and burn charts are common tactics, not prescribed Scrum artifacts or required measures.
    • Kanban: the current guide uses the exact flow metrics WIP, throughput, work item age, and cycle time and requires a Definition of Workflow. It does not require Scrum events.
    • Hybrid: adaptive measures may coexist with milestones, baselines, EVM, contracts, release governance, and transition dates. Do not combine unlike units without understanding their purpose.

    See Adaptive and Hybrid Delivery, Reserves and Baselines, and Earned Value Management.

    Common exam traps

    1. Unfinished story points do not count toward velocity.
    2. Velocity is neither comparable across teams nor an individual productivity metric.
    3. Story points are not hours.
    4. Burndown shows remaining work; burnup shows completed work and total scope.
    5. A rising burndown may mean scope was added.
    6. A widening cumulative-flow band can signal a bottleneck.
    7. Throughput counts completed items, not points.
    8. A predictive schedule lead overlaps predecessor and successor activities; procurement lead time is the time needed to obtain an external item or service.
    9. Adaptive lead time normally begins before active work; cycle time starts when work begins.
    10. Work item age applies only to started, unfinished work. WIP is a count or state, not elapsed time.
    11. Lead time is at least cycle time only when both share a finish and lead time starts earlier. Always identify the question’s boundaries.
    12. A board alone does not establish Kanban.
    13. Daily Scrum is not a manager status meeting, and no Scrum event requires a chart.
    14. Charts support decisions; they do not replace conversation and judgment.

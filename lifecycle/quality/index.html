    Lifecycle chapter 5

    Quality

    Quality connects requirements to planned criteria, capable processes, inspection evidence, verified deliverables, and acceptance decisions.

    Quality means meeting requirements

    Project quality is the degree to which a result and the processes used to create it satisfy stated requirements. Grade is a category or rank assigned to products that have the same general use but different features or characteristics. A lower-grade product can still be high quality if it meets its requirements; a high-grade product is poor quality if it fails them.

    Quality planning decides which standards apply, what evidence will demonstrate conformance, how work will be reviewed or tested, who performs each check, and what happens when results fail. Quality metrics make expectations measurable. Acceptance criteria state the conditions a particular result must satisfy for an authorized stakeholder to accept it.

    Planning, assurance, control, and acceptance

    Plan Quality Management establishes the quality approach and records it in the Quality Management Plan. It also defines metrics and connects requirements to verification and acceptance evidence.

    Manage Quality examines whether the project’s processes and quality approach are suitable and being followed. Audits, reviews, analysis, and improvement work can reveal causes that inspection alone would miss.

    Control Quality inspects or tests completed work. When the evidence shows that a deliverable conforms, its state becomes verified. If it fails, the team records the result and routes correction through the appropriate defect and change process.

    Validate Scope is different. It asks the authorized customer or acceptance role to make the formal acceptance decision using the verified deliverable and the agreed acceptance conditions. A quality team may verify technical conformance without having authority to accept the result for the customer.

    For ServiceBridge, an accessibility test can verify that the portal meets WCAG criteria. The customer-service acceptance authority then decides whether the verified portal satisfies the agreed business and acceptance conditions. Passing the test supports acceptance; it is not the acceptance decision itself.

    Predictive, adaptive, and hybrid treatment

    Predictive work may plan formal inspections, audits, test stages, and acceptance gates. Adaptive teams build quality into small increments through clear acceptance criteria, automation, review, and a Definition of Done. The Definition of Done is a shared quality commitment for an increment; it is not a substitute for every product-specific acceptance criterion or external approval.

    Hybrid work may use automated iterative testing inside formal compliance, contract, release, and customer-acceptance gates. The evidence can be produced continuously even when the final authority remains outside the team.

    For the exam, separate quality from grade, prevention from inspection, verification from acceptance, a verified deliverable from an accepted deliverable, and the Definition of Done from item-level acceptance criteria.

    Technical Practices That Support Adaptive Delivery

    These framework-neutral practices can shorten quality feedback and reduce delivery risk when the product, tooling, and team context support them. They are not required Scrum events and are not mandatory in every industry.

    Spike

    A time-limited investigation with an explicit question or learning objective. Its result may be knowledge, a prototype, a decision, or revised estimates—not production-ready functionality. Stop at the limit; do not let it become indefinite research.

    Pairing

    Two people collaborate closely on one work item. Rapid review can share knowledge, prevent defects, and reduce single-person dependency. It is optional, not a prescription for every task.

    Swarming

    Several team members focus on finishing or unblocking a high-priority item rather than starting more work. The purpose is lower WIP and earlier completion, not uncontrolled staffing.

    Continuous integration

    Changes are integrated frequently and verified rapidly so integration problems appear early and large late merges are reduced. Continuous integration does not automatically mean continuous deployment.

    Automated testing

    Repeatable checks can protect regression behavior, speed feedback, and make change safer. Automation complements rather than eliminates exploratory, usability, specialist, and other manual testing.

    TDD and ATDD

    Test-driven development uses short test-and-implementation feedback cycles. Acceptance test-driven development clarifies expected behavior collaboratively before implementation. Neither is a Scrum event, and automated acceptance tests do not constitute formal contractual acceptance.

    Refactoring

    Improve a solution’s internal structure without intentionally changing its external behavior. Done responsibly, it can reduce future delivery friction and make change safer.

    Technical debt

    Make accumulated consequences of expedient or deferred technical work visible. Debt can affect quality, speed, reliability, and maintainability; mitigate or repay it through risk, backlog, quality, and planning choices. It is neither a productivity score nor automatic evidence of misconduct.

    These practices connect to quality planning, prevention before inspection, item-level acceptance criteria, the shared Definition of Done where Scrum applies, continuous improvement, adaptive metrics, and risk reduction. A usable Increment must meet its Definition of Done; verification and formal acceptance remain separate decisions.

    For ServiceBridge, an integration spike answers whether the legacy identity interface can meet the response-time constraint. The result is an architecture decision and revised estimate, not a deployable feature. Later, automated regression checks expose a conflict between the portal and case-management service. The Product Owner can order technical-debt mitigation within product authority; a release, contract, or compliance effect follows its named decision route.

    Local sequence

    1. Use requirements, traceability, scope, risk, stakeholder, and compliance information to plan quality.
    2. Create the Quality Management Plan and Quality Metrics.
    3. Apply the planned approach during execution and improve process capability through Manage Quality.
    4. Inspect or test completed deliverables through Control Quality and record Quality-Control Measurements.
    5. Move conforming results to Verified Deliverables.
    6. Submit verified deliverables for formal acceptance, producing Accepted Deliverables when authorized acceptance is granted.

    Handoffs and return loops

    What enters

    Requirements, acceptance conditions, scope, standards, regulations, risk, stakeholder expectations, and lessons.

    What this work accomplishes

    Defines measurable quality, improves delivery processes, and produces evidence about deliverable conformance.

    What moves next

    Verified deliverables enter acceptance; defects, trends, and process findings return to execution, risk, planning, or change.

    Failed inspection does not become acceptance. The team analyzes the defect, determines the authorized response, performs correction or change work, and reinspects. A stakeholder rejection after verification is different: it may reveal an expectation, scope, requirement, or acceptance problem and follows the quality inspection failure decision guide as applicable.

    Quality tools and visual analysis

    Quality work runs on evidence, and most quality evidence starts as a pile of raw observations that no one can act on yet. The tools below turn that pile into a decision. They are worth learning as a sequence rather than as seven unrelated pictures, because each one answers the question the previous one leaves open.

    A team collects observations with a check sheet, looks at how the measurements are spread using a histogram, finds the few categories causing most of the trouble with a Pareto chart, explores why those categories happen with a cause-and-effect diagram, checks whether two things move together using a scatter diagram, watches whether the process stays stable over time on a control chart, and thinks ahead through what could go wrong using a process decision program chart.

    The split the exam cares about is where each tool sits. Data gathering is not data analysis, and neither is root-cause exploration. A check sheet gathers. A histogram, Pareto chart, scatter diagram, and control chart represent what was gathered. A cause-and-effect diagram and a PDPC explore causes and failure paths that the data alone cannot supply.

    1. Check sheetWhat did we actually observe?
    2. HistogramHow are the measurements spread?
    3. Pareto chartWhich few categories cause most of it?
    4. Cause-and-effect diagramWhat might be causing this problem?
    5. Scatter diagramDo these two variables move together?
    6. Control chartIs the process stable over time?
    7. Process decision program chartWhat could go wrong, and what would we do?

    The course groups these by intent. Quality assurance is proactive and process-oriented — it tries to prevent defects — and leans on process audits, cause-and-effect diagrams, and PDPCs. Quality control is reactive and product-oriented — it finds defects in what was produced — and leans on inspection, statistical sampling, check sheets, histograms, and control charts. The same chart can serve both; what changes is whether you are improving the process or judging the result.

    Check sheet

    A check sheet is a simple tally form for counting things as they happen. Someone decides the categories in advance, then marks one stroke each time an occurrence is observed. That is the whole tool, and its simplicity is the point: it produces trustworthy counts because recording happens at the moment of observation rather than from memory later.

    Use it whenever you need real frequency data — defect types found in testing, reasons a call was escalated, causes of rework. Everything further down this section depends on it, because a histogram or Pareto chart drawn from guessed numbers is just a decorated guess.

    Read it by comparing counts across categories, and check that the categories cover everything you might see — an overflowing "Other" row usually means the categories were chosen badly.

    Exam note. A check sheet collects data; it does not analyze it. Do not confuse it with a checklist, which confirms that required steps or items were completed. One counts occurrences, the other confirms completion.

    Histogram

    A histogram is a bar chart showing how often measurements fall into each range, so you can see the shape of a set of data instead of a single average. Bars sit in natural order — smallest range to largest — and touch, because the ranges are continuous.

    Use it when you want to know how much variation exists and where the results cluster. An average of 1.8 seconds tells you very little; a histogram showing a long tail out past three seconds tells you some users are having a much worse experience than the average suggests.

    Original Atlas illustration. Most responses land between 1.5 and 2.5 seconds, with a thin tail of slow results worth investigating.

    Read it for three things: where the results center, how wide the spread is, and whether the shape is lopsided or has a tail. Bars in a histogram are not ranked by size — their order is fixed by the measurement scale.

    Exam note. Histogram versus Pareto chart is a favorite distinction. A histogram shows a frequency distribution in natural order and answers "how are results spread?" A Pareto chart ranks categories from largest to smallest and answers "which ones should we fix first?" A Pareto chart is a specific kind of histogram, not the other way round.

    Pareto chart

    A Pareto chart is a bar chart of categories ordered from most frequent to least, with a line showing the running cumulative percentage. It exists to answer one question: where will fixing effort do the most good?

    It reflects the 80/20 idea — a large share of the problems usually traces back to a small share of the causes. Use it after a check sheet, when you have real counts by category and limited capacity to fix things.

    Original Atlas illustration, built from the check-sheet counts above. The cumulative line crosses 80% at the third category, so three defect types explain most of the week's defects.

    Read the bars left to right and stop where the cumulative line crosses roughly 80%. Those categories are the vital few. Here, session timeouts alone account for about 41% of defects, so they earn the first investigation.

    Exam note. A Pareto chart tells you where the problems concentrate. It does not tell you why — that is the next tool's job — and it says nothing about severity. Ten cosmetic defects are not automatically more important than one that corrupts data.

    Cause-and-Effect Diagram

    A Cause-and-Effect Diagram organizes the possible causes of one problem into categories so a team can explore them systematically instead of arguing about whichever cause comes to mind first. It is also called a Fishbone Diagram because of its shape, or an Ishikawa Diagram after its originator.

    Use it once you have chosen a problem worth solving — typically the top bar of a Pareto chart. The problem statement goes in the head, category branches come off the spine, and specific candidate causes hang off each branch. The learner-friendly 5M+E model uses Manpower / People, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, and Environment. It corresponds closely to the traditional manufacturing-oriented “6M” formulation, using Environment for the environmental category. PMI does not mandate one universal category set; teams may tailor branches to the problem and context.

    Original Atlas illustration. The 5M+E branches organize candidate causes; evidence must still confirm which cause is real.

    Read it as a map of hypotheses, not findings. The branches show where to look; testing or data then confirms which cause is real. Teams often reach the real cause by asking "why?" repeatedly down one branch.

    Exam note. Every cause on the diagram is a candidate until evidence supports it. The diagram supports root-cause analysis; it is not itself proof of a root cause.

    Scatter diagram

    A scatter diagram plots pairs of values on two axes — one variable across, one up — so you can see whether they appear to move together. Each dot is one observation measured on both variables.

    Use it when you suspect a relationship and want to check it before acting. If timeouts really do rise with the number of concurrent users, capacity is a plausible cause; if the dots form a shapeless cloud, the theory needs rethinking.

    Original Atlas illustration. The upward pattern suggests a relationship worth testing; it does not establish one.

    Read the pattern, not the individual dots. Points rising left to right suggest a positive relationship, falling points a negative one, and a formless scatter suggests no useful relationship at this scale.

    Exam note. Correlation is not causation. A scatter diagram can show that two variables move together; it cannot prove that one causes the other, and a third factor may be driving both. Do not confuse it with a control chart either — a scatter diagram compares two variables, while a control chart tracks one variable over time.

    Control chart

    A control chart plots one measurement over time against a center line and upper and lower control limits, so you can tell whether a process is behaving predictably. Control limits describe how the process actually performs; specification limits state what the customer or requirement demands, and the two are not the same thing.

    Use it for repetitive processes that produce measurements often enough to show a pattern — build times, defect rates, response times, call handling times.

    Original Atlas illustration. Nine days of ordinary variation and one day that behaves differently.

    Read it by asking whether the process looks stable. Points wandering randomly inside the limits reflect common-cause variation — the normal noise the process always produces, which you change only by changing the process itself. A point outside a limit, or a long run of points on one side, suggests special-cause variation: something specific happened on that day, and it is worth investigating. The Atlas covers the same chart applied to cycle time in Adaptive Metrics and Visual Management.

    Exam note. Being inside the control limits does not mean the customer is satisfied — that is what specification limits are for. And the chart flags that something changed; it never tells you what changed.

    Process decision program chart (PDPC)

    A process decision program chart maps a planned activity into the things that could go wrong at each step and the countermeasure you would use for each one. It is forward-looking: you use it before the work, while there is still time to prevent trouble or to prepare a response.

    Use it for high-stakes steps where failure would be expensive or hard to reverse — a data migration, a cutover, a regulated submission, a single-shot integration test.

    Original Atlas illustration. Countermeasures decided in advance become part of the plan rather than an improvisation during the cutover.

    Read it downward: the step, then each realistic failure, then the response you have already agreed. Anything without a workable countermeasure is a signal to change the plan rather than proceed and hope.

    Exam note. A PDPC and a cause-and-effect diagram point in opposite directions in time. A cause-and-effect diagram looks backward from a problem that already exists to find why. A PDPC looks forward from a planned activity to what might go wrong and what you would do. A PDPC also overlaps with risk work — the difference is that it stays anchored to the steps of one planned activity.

    Roles, authority, and approaches

    The project manager ensures integration. Quality specialists and the delivery team plan and perform quality work. Control Quality verifies conformance. The designated customer or sponsor role accepts; quality personnel do not silently substitute for that authority.

    Predictive work may establish broader criteria and planned reviews before production. Adaptive work embeds criteria, review, testing, and feedback in each increment. Hybrid work must maintain consistent evidence and acceptance authority across both cadences.

    Exam distinctions

    Manage Quality concerns the effectiveness and improvement of processes; Control Quality concerns results and conformance evidence. Verification precedes acceptance. Grade and quality are different concepts. Commercial audit or template examples are illustrations, not PMI-prescribed formats; detailed audit claims remain source-limited where the registry lacks current primary verification.

    Among the quality tools: a check sheet gathers data while the charts represent it; a histogram shows a frequency distribution in natural order while a Pareto chart ranks categories to prioritize them; a scatter diagram compares two variables while a control chart tracks one variable over time; correlation on a scatter diagram is not proof of causation; control limits describe the process while specification limits state the requirement; common-cause variation is the process's normal noise while special-cause variation signals that something specific happened; and a cause-and-effect diagram looks backward for why a problem occurred while a process decision program chart looks forward at what could go wrong.

    Lifecycle chapter 6

    Risk, Compliance, and Procurement

    Uncertainty, mandatory obligations, and external commitments affect one another and must remain visible from planning through closure.

    Risk is uncertainty; an issue exists now

    A risk is an uncertain event or condition that could affect project objectives. It may be a threat or an opportunity. An issue is a condition that already exists and requires action or a decision. A data-migration failure that might occur is a risk; a failed migration discovered in today’s test is an issue.

    Risk management gives uncertainty a repeatable path. The team decides how risk will be managed, identifies uncertainties, assesses priority, performs quantitative analysis when useful, plans responses, implements approved actions, and monitors changes. The Risk Register holds individual risks and their owners, triggers, responses, and status. The Risk Report summarizes overall exposure and trends for decision-makers.

    Threat responses include avoiding, mitigating, transferring, escalating, or accepting the threat. Opportunity responses include exploiting, enhancing, sharing, escalating, or accepting the opportunity. A response is useful only when a person owns it, the timing is clear, and the team can recognize when action is required.

    Compliance changes project work

    Compliance is not a final inspection added after delivery. A law, regulation, policy, standard, license, or contract can change requirements, design choices, evidence, supplier terms, testing, acceptance authority, record retention, and transition conditions.

    For ServiceBridge, accessibility and retention obligations become requirements. They affect design, testing, procurement clauses, acceptance evidence, and operational records. If the team discovers that a requirement cannot be met, the issue follows the applicable risk, change, compliance, and governance route; the project manager does not unilaterally waive it.

    The July 2026 ECO explicitly includes sustainability among possible compliance requirements and sustainability risk among risks that may require a response. Classify the actual obligation or uncertainty instead of assuming every sustainability concern is the same: a binding emissions limit is a compliance requirement; uncertain future water availability may be a risk; a desired reduction in operating impact may be a value or quality objective. Record the owner, evidence, threshold, response, and governance route appropriate to that classification.

    Source alignment. Sustainability coverage maps ECO Business Environment Tasks 2 and 5, PDF page 11 (SRC-PMI-ECO-JULY-2026). The classification examples are Atlas synthesis; they do not assert a PMI-prescribed taxonomy.

    Procurement connects project needs to seller obligations

    Procurement planning decides what to obtain externally, how the market will be approached, which contract type fits the uncertainty, how sellers will be evaluated, and who may make commercial decisions. Conduct Procurements obtains and evaluates seller responses and establishes an agreement. Control Procurements monitors performance, manages claims and changes, and closes the commercial obligations.

    Contract type allocates financial risk differently. Fixed-price arrangements place more cost risk on the seller when the scope is sufficiently defined. Cost-reimbursable arrangements place more cost risk on the buyer and require suitable oversight. Time-and-materials arrangements combine fixed rates with variable quantities and need clear limits.

    The project manager coordinates scope, schedule, risk, technical evidence, and stakeholder effects, but only authorized buyer and seller representatives can bind their organizations or change the agreement.

    Predictive procurement often defines detailed deliverables before award. Adaptive or hybrid procurement may use outcome-based scope, short delivery increments, demonstrations, and adjustable backlogs while retaining funding limits, acceptance conditions, compliance duties, and formal contract-change authority.

    On the exam, distinguish risk from issue, a planned response from an implemented response, residual risk from secondary risk, project change control from contract change, and seller performance management from informal team direction.

    Local sequence

    1. Plan the risk approach, identify uncertainties, and build the Risk Register.
    2. Prioritize risks and perform quantitative analysis when warranted and supported.
    3. Select responses, assign owners, implement agreed actions, and monitor residual and emerging risk.
    4. Trace compliance obligations into requirements, scope, quality criteria, risk, procurement terms, acceptance evidence, and closure records.
    5. Plan procurement, prepare solicitation information, select sellers through authorized conduct, and establish agreements.
    6. Control seller performance, changes, claims, records, and completion; close contractual obligations through the applicable authority.

    Handoffs

    What enters

    Objectives, scope, assumptions, constraints, compliance obligations, market information, risk appetite, and make-or-buy considerations.

    What this work accomplishes

    Makes uncertainty actionable, obligations traceable, and external commitments governable.

    What moves next

    Responses, owners, procurement decisions, agreements, controls, and evidence enter execution, monitoring, acceptance, and closure.

    Feedback, issues, and authority

    A risk trigger can create response work, an issue, or a change request. A compliance finding can block acceptance or require corrective action. Seller performance can change schedule, cost, quality, and risk. These paths return through domain control and integrated change rather than bypassing authority.

    Risk owners monitor assigned uncertainties; response owners perform agreed actions. Procurement authority depends on organizational policy and agreement terms. Compliance authority depends on the governing obligation. The project manager integrates impacts but does not invent authority beyond the project governance model.

    Adaptive procurement and contracting

    Traditional fixed scope can conflict with iterative discovery and changing priorities. The response is not to remove governance, but to decide what is fixed, what may vary, who can decide, how acceptance works, and where risk sits. This is instructional project-management guidance, not legal advice or contract language. Applicable law, organizational procurement rules, regulation, auditability, funding constraints, risk allocation, seller capability, trust, transparency, and acceptance authority remain controlling.

    Illustrative structure What is fixed What may vary Decision authority and useful conditions Major risk
    Fixed scope / fixed price Defined scope, price, obligations, and acceptance basis Only authorized changes Procurement and contract authorities control binding terms; useful when needs are stable and objectively definable Discovery can create disputes or costly change
    Time and materials Rates, commercial rules, and any authorized cap Quantity and evolving work within guardrails Authorized buyer controls funding and continuation; useful when effort is uncertain and progress can be reviewed often Buyer cost exposure and weak outcome focus
    Incremental or staged Master terms, each authorization, acceptance criteria, review points Later increments, ordering, or funding stages Procurement and acceptance authorities approve stages; useful when value can be delivered and evaluated incrementally Fragmentation, interface gaps, and repeated coordination
    Hybrid structure Stable infrastructure, security, compliance, funding, or transition obligations User-facing priorities inside explicit limits Named commercial, product, compliance, and acceptance authorities act within their domains; useful when stable obligations and uncertain solution detail coexist Ambiguous boundaries or conflicting incentives

    Source-supported patterns include separating stable master terms from changeable work authorizations, emphasizing delivered value, and using smaller fixed-price increments. Other arrangements may use shorter horizons, staged funding, capped time and materials, shared incentives, or early exit points only when authorized terms actually establish them. No pattern is universally preferred.

    For ServiceBridge, infrastructure, security, retention, and transition obligations can remain fixed while user-facing software priorities evolve. Staged delivery reviews examine usable results against objective criteria. The Product Owner may reprioritize inside the authorized software scope; only the procurement authority can alter contractual obligations, and the customer or named acceptance authority makes the applicable acceptance decision.

    Source alignment. Lesson 6 PDF pages 129–137 directly covers broad contract families, including adaptive and hybrid contracts, and procurement authority. Compatible older PMI depth comes from Agile Practice Guide §6.3, PDF pages 92–94 (printed pages 77–79). The comparison and ServiceBridge structure are illustrative Atlas synthesis, not recommended legal terms.

    Approach treatment and exam distinctions

    Predictive work may establish broader risk and procurement structures early. Adaptive work reviews uncertainty and obligations frequently as increments evolve. Hybrid work must connect adaptive discovery to contract, baseline, and compliance controls.

    A risk is uncertain; an issue has occurred. A contingency response differs from an unapproved scope change. Contract type influences risk allocation but does not eliminate buyer oversight. Compliance is not a closing checklist: it must be traceable from requirements through evidence and handoff. Detailed contract and audit claims remain in the process references or deferred until current primary sources support them.

    Adaptive contracting still requires defined acceptance, decision authority, governance, auditability, and risk allocation. Reprioritizing work does not authorize a contract change. No contract pattern is universal.

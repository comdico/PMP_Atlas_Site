    Scrum Events and Adaptive Cadences

    A cadence creates repeated opportunities to inspect and adapt

    Scrum events are formal opportunities for the Scrum Team to create transparency, inspect results, and adapt. Other adaptive approaches use different cadences for planning, replenishment, delivery, review, and improvement. A cadence exists to make feedback and decisions frequent enough to reduce risk; it is not a calendar of status meetings.

    The Sprint contains the other Scrum events and produces a usable Increment toward the Sprint Goal. Sprint Planning establishes why the Sprint is valuable, what can be done, and how the selected work will be delivered. The Daily Scrum lets Developers inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan. Sprint Review examines the outcome with stakeholders and may change future Product Backlog ordering. Sprint Retrospective examines team effectiveness and selects improvements. Backlog refinement is an ongoing activity, not an official Scrum event. Kanban cadences and flow reviews should be described by their own system rather than relabeled as Scrum events.

    For ServiceBridge, a Sprint Review shows users abandoning an appointment step. The Product Owner uses the evidence to reorder a usability improvement ahead of a lower-value report. In the Retrospective, the team separately finds that late accessibility checks caused rework and changes its working agreement. One event changes product direction; the other improves the way the team works. Hybrid governance may use the same evidence at a release or compliance gate without turning the Sprint Review into formal project acceptance. On the exam, identify what is being inspected—goal progress, product outcome, or team effectiveness—before choosing the event.

    What people often call “Agile ceremonies,” what Scrum actually defines, and how other adaptive approaches differ.

    Terminology first

    Agile ceremonies is common informal language. Agile itself does not prescribe one universal set of meetings. Scrum officially calls its five timeboxed elements events: the Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, and Sprint Retrospective. The Sprint contains the other four events. Product Backlog refinement is important ongoing activity, but it is not a formal Scrum event. Other adaptive approaches can use similar cadences without following Scrum, and Kanban does not require the Scrum event structure.

    The five Scrum events

    Official Scrum event

    Sprint

    Official purpose
    Create value through a usable Increment while progressing toward the Product Goal.
    Participants
    Scrum Team
    When
    A new Sprint begins immediately after the previous Sprint concludes.
    Maximum timebox
    Fixed length of one month or less. The Sprint retains a fixed length of one month or less; the contained events are usually shorter.
    Primary inputs
    Product Goal, Product Backlog, Definition of Done
    Outputs or adaptations
    Increment, Product Backlog
    Related roles
    Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers

    What happens

    During the Sprint, the Sprint Goal is not endangered, quality does not decrease, and scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the Product Owner as learning occurs. Work needed to achieve the Product Goal happens within Sprints. Only the Product Owner can cancel a Sprint, and only when the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete.

    Common misunderstanding: A Sprint is not merely a development phase. The Product Owner alone may cancel a Sprint when the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete.

    PMP-style distinction: Protect the Sprint Goal and quality; clarify and renegotiate scope with the Product Owner as learning occurs.

    Hybrid-project treatment

    The Sprint governs adaptive delivery work; project funding, contracts, external milestones, compliance, and transition dates remain in the surrounding governance model.

    Official Scrum event

    Sprint Planning

    Official purpose
    Decide why the Sprint is valuable, what can be completed, and how the selected work will be approached.
    Participants
    Entire Scrum Team
    When
    Starts the Sprint.
    Maximum timebox
    Maximum eight hours for a one-month Sprint. Usually shorter for shorter Sprints.
    Primary inputs
    Product Goal, Product Backlog, Definition of Done
    Outputs or adaptations
    Sprint Goal, Sprint Backlog
    Related roles
    Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers

    What happens

    The Scrum Team addresses three topics: Why is this Sprint valuable? What can be done this Sprint? How will the chosen work get done? The Product Owner brings priorities and context. Developers select and plan work using capacity and past performance, and they are responsible for sizing it. The Product Owner or a project manager does not assign Developers' detailed work.

    Common misunderstanding: The Product Owner does not assign Developers' detailed work, Developers are responsible for sizing their work.

    PMP-style distinction: Remember the three topics: why valuable, what can be done, and how it will get done.

    Hybrid-project treatment

    The team plans Sprint work within project-level release, compliance, dependency, and milestone constraints.

    Official Scrum event

    Daily Scrum

    Official purpose
    Inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the immediate plan.
    Participants
    Developers
    When
    Every working day of the Sprint at the same time and place to reduce complexity.
    Maximum timebox
    Fifteen minutes. The maximum remains fifteen minutes.
    Primary inputs
    Sprint Goal, Sprint Backlog
    Outputs or adaptations
    Sprint Backlog
    Related roles
    Developers, Scrum Master, Product Owner

    What happens

    Developers may choose any structure that focuses on progress toward the Sprint Goal and produces an actionable plan. The former three-question format is optional. Additional discussions can follow. Product Owner or Scrum Master attendance does not convert the event into a status meeting for them.

    Common misunderstanding: The old three-question format is optional. Product Owner or Scrum Master attendance does not make this their status meeting.

    PMP-style distinction: A manager asking each person for status is not the purpose of the Daily Scrum.

    Hybrid-project treatment

    Project reporting consumes transparent Sprint information without turning the Daily Scrum into a manager-led status meeting.

    Official Scrum event

    Sprint Review

    Official purpose
    Inspect the Sprint outcome with stakeholders and decide what to do next.
    Participants
    Scrum Team, Relevant stakeholders
    When
    Near the end of the Sprint before the Sprint Retrospective.
    Maximum timebox
    Maximum four hours for a one-month Sprint. Usually shorter for shorter Sprints.
    Primary inputs
    Product Goal, Product Backlog, Increment
    Outputs or adaptations
    Product Backlog
    Related roles
    Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers, Stakeholder, Customer or Acceptance Authority

    What happens

    The Scrum Team and key stakeholders inspect the outcome, progress toward the Product Goal, and relevant environmental or market changes. They collaborate on what to do next and may adapt the Product Backlog. It is a working session, not merely a presentation, demo, or ceremonial sign-off.

    Common misunderstanding: It is a working session rather than merely a demo. It is not automatically formal acceptance.

    PMP-style distinction: Focus on product outcome, Product Goal progress, environmental change, stakeholder feedback, and next steps.

    Hybrid-project treatment

    Review feedback may adapt the Product Backlog but does not automatically approve baseline change or constitute contractual acceptance.

    Official Scrum event

    Sprint Retrospective

    Official purpose
    Increase quality and team effectiveness.
    Participants
    Entire Scrum Team
    When
    Concludes the Sprint after the Sprint Review.
    Maximum timebox
    Maximum three hours for a one-month Sprint. Usually shorter for shorter Sprints.
    Primary inputs
    Sprint Goal, Sprint Backlog, Definition of Done
    Outputs or adaptations
    Sprint Backlog, Definition of Done
    Related roles
    Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers

    What happens

    The Scrum Team inspects people, interactions, processes, tools, and the Definition of Done. It selects the most useful improvements, which may enter the next Sprint Backlog. The event is not a blame session, performance appraisal, or product-acceptance review.

    Common misunderstanding: It is not a blame session or performance appraisal. It examines how the team works rather than stakeholder satisfaction with the product.

    PMP-style distinction: Inspect people, interactions, processes, tools, and Definition of Done; select useful improvements.

    Hybrid-project treatment

    Team improvements enter the next Sprint while project-level lessons, quality, risk, and governance records remain coordinated separately.

    Review versus Retrospective

    Distinction Sprint Review Sprint Retrospective
    Primary focus Product outcome and progress toward the Product Goal Quality and how the Scrum Team works
    Participants Scrum Team and relevant stakeholders Entire Scrum Team
    Typical adaptation Product Backlog, direction, or next steps People, interactions, processes, tools, Definition of Done, or next-Sprint improvement
    Not the purpose Merely demonstrating work or automatic contractual acceptance Blame, performance appraisal, or deciding whether stakeholders like the product

    Backlog refinement: important, but not a Scrum event

    Refinement can clarify items, split large items, add detail, revise order, estimate or size work, discuss acceptance conditions, and remove obsolete items. It occurs as needed throughout the Sprint. A team may use a Definition of Ready to support this conversation. The Product Owner remains accountable for effective Product Backlog management, while Developers are responsible for sizing the work they will perform. Scrum prescribes neither a required refinement meeting nor a fixed cadence; a scheduled session is a team practice, not a sixth Scrum event.

    Definition of Ready (DoR)

    Definition of Ready asks: Is this item ready for us to start?

    PMI's July 2026 PMP course explicitly teaches Definition of Ready (DoR) as a team checklist for deciding whether a user-centric requirement has enough information for work to begin. In practice, a team can use a DoR—sometimes described informally as ready criteria—as an agreed set of conditions during adaptive planning or Product Backlog refinement. Its value is the conversation: the team and relevant stakeholders can expose missing information, dependencies, or preparation before making a near-term delivery commitment.

    The flow is Product Backlog → refinement → Definition of Ready, when used → iteration or Sprint Backlog. The words when used matter. A Definition of Ready is a contextual team practice, not a prescribed Scrum artifact, event, or commitment, and Scrum does not require every team to have one. “Ready” also does not mean eliminating every uncertainty before work begins; adaptive work still expects learning after the team starts.

    Keep the three questions separate

    1. Definition of Ready: Is the item ready to start?
    2. Definition of Done: Is the completed Increment actually done to the shared quality commitment?
    3. Acceptance criteria: What must this particular item or deliverable satisfy?

    For example, a ServiceBridge backlog item may not be ready if the user need is still unclear, its acceptance criteria are missing, a required dependency is unknown, or the team lacks enough information to begin responsibly. Those are examples for discussion, not a universal mandatory checklist.

    Adaptive cadences beyond Scrum

    Adaptive teams may use the following contextual practices. They are not universal mandatory ceremonies, and their purpose matters more than copying a meeting name.

    • Release planning
    • Roadmap review
    • Iteration planning
    • Customer feedback sessions
    • Technical design discussions
    • Risk review
    • Dependency coordination
    • Demonstrations
    • Communities of practice
    • Improvement workshops

    Kanban does not prescribe the Scrum event cycle

    The minimal Kanban Guide emphasizes:

    • Define and visualize the workflow
    • Actively manage work items
    • Control work in progress
    • Examine flow metrics
    • Improve the workflow

    Kanban teams may use regular cadences or make just-in-time workflow changes. The minimal guide does not require Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, or Sprint Retrospective. A team may combine Scrum events with Kanban practices, but it should label that combination accurately rather than calling every flow meeting a Scrum event.

    Scrum artifacts and commitments

    These concepts are defined here as the Atlas's focused adaptive-artifact reference. They do not expand the predictive 41-artifact inventory.

    Product Goal

    The long-term objective for the Scrum Team and the commitment for the Product Backlog.

    Primary ownership: Product Owner

    Product Backlog

    An emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product.

    Primary ownership: Product Owner

    Sprint Goal

    The single objective for the Sprint and the commitment for the Sprint Backlog.

    Primary ownership: Developers, Product Owner, Scrum Master

    Sprint Backlog

    The Sprint Goal, selected Product Backlog items, and the Developers' actionable delivery plan.

    Primary ownership: Developers

    Increment

    A concrete stepping stone toward the Product Goal that is usable and meets the Definition of Done.

    Primary ownership: Developers

    Definition of Done

    A formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets required product quality measures.

    Primary ownership: Developers

    Reserved project decisions

    Scrum delivery cadence

    Adaptive software delivery

    Sprint Planning, delivery with Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective, and ongoing Product Backlog refinement.

    Sprint Planning controls iteration work; Daily Scrum coordinates Developers; Sprint Review gathers stakeholder feedback; and Sprint Retrospective improves delivery. Project milestone, vendor, risk, budget, and governance reviews continue outside Scrum. Sprint Review does not replace formal baseline change approval or contractual acceptance. Project reporting should use Scrum information without turning Scrum events into management status meetings. This arrangement is an Atlas hybrid synthesis, not an additional Scrum rule.

    Common exam traps

    1. Daily Scrum is not a manager status meeting.
    2. Sprint Review is not merely a demo and is not automatically formal acceptance.
    3. Sprint Retrospective improves quality and team effectiveness.
    4. Product Backlog refinement is not an official Scrum event.
    5. The former three Daily Scrum questions are not mandatory.
    6. The Product Owner orders value; Developers decide how to perform the work.
    7. The Scrum Master enables effectiveness and is not the team's task manager.
    8. Agile does not mean Scrum.
    9. Kanban does not require Scrum events.
    10. Hybrid governance meetings do not replace Scrum events, and Scrum events do not replace project governance.

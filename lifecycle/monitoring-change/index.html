    Lifecycle chapter 8

    Monitoring and Change

    Monitoring explains what current evidence means; change control decides what authorized direction should change.

    Monitoring turns observations into decisions

    Monitoring is not the same as collecting status data. Work performance data are raw observations such as dates, costs, defects, completed items, and test results. Analysis gives those observations context and produces work performance information. Selected information is then organized into work performance reports for stakeholders who need to understand status, forecasts, risks, or decisions.

    For example, “migration test 17 failed” is data. Comparing failures across test cycles and identifying a rising error rate produces information. A report that explains the trend, its forecast effect, and the decision required gives governance a usable view.

    Issues, impediments, and risks

    A risk is uncertain. An Issue Log records a condition that already exists, who owns the response, when action is due, and when escalation is required. An impediment is something blocking or slowing work. A team may remove a small impediment immediately; a material or persistent impediment may become a project issue and require escalation.

    The label matters because the response changes. Risks are analyzed before they occur. Issues need current action. Impediments need flow restored. The records may link to one another, but they should not hide the current state of the problem.

    A change moves through distinct states

    A Change Request is a proposal. It explains what should change, why, and what approved commitments may be affected. Impact analysis examines scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, risk, contracts, compliance, benefits, and stakeholder effects.

    The authorized change role then approves, rejects, defers, or requests more information. Approval changes the decision state; it does not implement the work. The approved change is communicated and assigned, the team implements it, affected plans and baselines are updated under control, and the result is verified. The Change Log preserves that trace.

    This sequence prevents four different things from being collapsed into one word:

    1. requested change;
    2. approved change;
    3. implemented change;
    4. verified change.

    For ServiceBridge, a request to strengthen encryption is analyzed for design, cost, schedule, seller, and compliance effects. The change authority approves it. The delivery team implements it. Security testing verifies the result. Only then can the log show both the decision and its completed implementation evidence.

    Adaptive teams may reprioritize uncommitted backlog detail without a formal baseline change. A change to a controlled release, contract, compliance obligation, funding limit, or other approved commitment still follows the relevant authority route. Hybrid projects must make that boundary explicit.

    For the exam, assess first, follow the approved change process, update affected records after the decision, and verify the implemented result. Urgency does not give the project manager unlimited authority.

    Local sequence

    1. Capture work performance data from execution and relevant external conditions.
    2. Analyze domain results into Work Performance Information.
    3. Integrate status, variance, trends, forecasts, risks, issues, and dependencies through Monitor and Control Project Work.
    4. Communicate decision-ready Work Performance Reports.
    5. Resolve issues within delegated authority or escalate through the named governance route.
    6. Document proposed changes as Change Requests, analyze impacts, and obtain an authorized disposition.
    7. Implement approved changes, update controlled records, and verify the resulting work.

    Handoffs and return loops

    What enters

    Actual results, forecasts, defects, risks, issues, stakeholder feedback, agreement performance, and current plans.

    What this work accomplishes

    Creates integrated understanding and authorized responses while preserving configuration and decision history.

    What moves next

    Actions return to execution; approved changes update affected owners; rejected requests leave baselines unchanged; verified results proceed to acceptance.

    Control is continuous. A defect can return to correction and reinspection. A variance can prompt analysis, corrective action, or change. A new issue can reveal a risk response failure. Stakeholder feedback can require engagement adjustment without necessarily changing scope. Every return path needs an owner and an authority boundary.

    From work performed to an authorized action

    Charts do not create knowledge; work does. Every measure and every visual on this project sits somewhere in one sequence, and knowing where it sits explains what it can and cannot settle.

    1. Work is performed.
    2. Operational data is generated: an item moves, a test runs, a cost is incurred, a blocker appears.
    3. The data is recorded in the system that owns it — the board, the backlog tool, the test system, the cost system, a log or register.
    4. The data is interpreted as information by comparing it with a plan, a baseline, a target, a history, or a workflow expectation.
    5. The information is communicated or summarized for the audience that needs it.
    6. The team or the authorized decision-maker reviews it.
    7. A corrective, preventive, improvement, escalation, backlog, or change action may follow — or the evidence may support taking no action at all.

    The measures and visuals the Atlas defines in Adaptive Metrics and Visual Management occupy the middle of that sequence. The tables below place them in the project's information flow rather than repeating their formulas, chart mechanics, or boundary rules.

    A measure is not its interpretation, and an interpretation is not a decision. A burnup that shows scope rising is evidence; whether that becomes a backlog reordering, a release-content decision, or a formal change request depends on which approved commitment the growth touches. A control chart signals that something merits investigation without naming the cause. An impediment view supports escalation precisely when the team cannot remove the blocker within its own authority. A project report may legitimately draw on data from adaptive delivery systems, but reporting to governance is not a reason to convert the Daily Scrum into a management status meeting; the report is a derived view, and the delivery system remains the record.

    Reviewing the external business environment

    Project status includes conditions outside the project, not only performance against the current plan. Continually survey regulatory, technological, geopolitical, market, economic, social, and environmental change. PESTLE, TECOP, and VUCA are prompts for scanning; they do not replace analysis. Feasibility evidence, SWOT, assumptions, history, comparative advantage, and alignment with organizational risk and strategy can provide additional context.

    When an external change appears, determine whether it creates a threat, opportunity, compliance obligation, changed value assumption, or changed stakeholder need. Assess and prioritize its effect on scope or the product backlog. An adaptive team may reorder uncommitted work within delegated product authority; an effect on a controlled baseline, contract, funding boundary, or regulatory commitment must enter the relevant governance and change process. Continue reviewing after the first response because external conditions and their consequences can evolve.

The placement in Monitoring and Change is an Atlas synthesis; the ECO, not this lifecycle chapter, remains the exam's organizing structure.

    Issues, impediments, and configuration

    An issue is a current condition requiring action; a risk is uncertain. An impediment blocks or slows progress and may be an issue, organizational constraint, dependency, or policy concern. The lifecycle guide makes these relationships explicit, but dedicated issue-log and impediment destinations remain deferred because current repository sources do not yet support responsible standalone pages.

    An issue is also not a change request. Most issues are resolved by acting within existing authority and recording the resolution. An issue becomes a change request only when the response would alter an approved commitment — scope, schedule, cost, quality, contract, compliance, or another controlled document.

    Version and configuration controls preserve which approved artifact state applies. A changed working document, an approved change, and an updated baseline are different events. The systems that hold that evidence — the PMIS, artifact repositories, version control, and configuration management — are explained in Project information and artifact control; control during monitoring is the act of deciding which recorded state is the authorized one and keeping that decision traceable.

    Change control across predictive, adaptive, and hybrid delivery

    Every delivery approach accepts that projects change. They differ in which decisions the team may make on its own, which require a named authority, and what has to be recorded afterward.

    Predictive change control

    Predictive change control asks three questions about any proposed change: what kind of change is it, who decides, and what has to be updated once it is done.

    Four types of change requests

    Every change request is one of four types: corrective action, preventive action, defect repair, or an update. The type describes what the request is trying to fix, which is why it is settled early — it points the analysis at the right evidence and often at the right approver.

    1. Corrective action brings the performance of the work back into line with the plan. Something has already drifted, and this realigns it.
    2. Preventive action reduces the chance that future work departs from the plan. Nothing has gone wrong yet; the team has seen a warning sign and acts before it does.
    3. Defect repair fixes a nonconformance in what the project produced — a component or deliverable that does not meet its requirement.
    4. Update changes a baseline or another controlled document, such as a plan or a contract, so the approved reference matches what was actually authorized.

    The fastest way to keep them apart: corrective and preventive action are about the work, defect repair is about the product, and an update is about the record. Corrective action answers a variance that has already happened; preventive action answers one that has not.

    One situation can produce several types at once. A failed migration test may generate a defect repair for the build, corrective action for the review step that let the defect through, and an update to the schedule baseline if the rework consumes float that was already committed.

    How a change request moves from need to updated record

    The route from need to closed record runs through distinct steps, and skipping one is how projects lose control of their own commitments:

    1. A need for change is identified, from any source and any stakeholder.
    2. The change is documented as a Change Request rather than agreed in conversation.
    3. Impact is analyzed across scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, risk, contracts, compliance, benefits, and stakeholders.
    4. The request is routed to the authorized decision-maker or change authority named in the change management approach.
    5. The request is approved, rejected, deferred, or otherwise dispositioned, and the disposition is recorded in the Change Log.
    6. An approved change is communicated, assigned, and implemented.
    7. The implementation is verified against what was approved.
    8. Affected plans, baselines, documents, configurations, and records are updated under control.

    What a change request does not automatically mean

    Six qualifications keep this from becoming a reflex:

    1. Not every issue requires a formal change request; acting within existing authority and recording the result is often the correct response.
    2. Not every update changes a baseline. Refreshing a register, a log, or a working document leaves the approved reference untouched.
    3. The project manager does not automatically hold authority to approve every change, and urgency does not create that authority.
    4. Change authority is often delegated by threshold — value, schedule effect, risk exposure, or category — so the same project can have several legitimate decision points.
    5. Contractual, regulatory, funding, or baseline effects can require the sponsor, a governance body, a change control board, procurement, the customer, or another named authority.
    6. Configuration control and integrated change control are related but not identical. Configuration control governs the identification and approved state of controlled items; integrated change control governs decisions about proposed changes and their effects across the whole project.

    Adaptive change control

    Adaptive work accepts change as a normal condition rather than an exception. That is not the same as uncontrolled change. The control is placed differently: in ordering, in commitments, and in quality boundaries rather than in a form and a board.

    1. Product Backlog refinement adds detail, order, size, and clarity to items as more is learned, continuously rather than at a gate.
    2. The Product Owner is accountable for ordering the Product Backlog, which is where most product-level change is expressed.
    3. Stakeholder feedback, especially at the Sprint Review, informs what the backlog should contain next.
    4. Developers hold authority over how the work is performed, including the technical response to what they learn.
    5. Iteration or Sprint Planning converts current order and current understanding into a commitment for one cycle.
    6. Clarification and renegotiation of scope detail continue as understanding improves; this is expected, not a failure of planning.
    7. The Sprint Goal is protected. Scope within a Sprint can be clarified and renegotiated between the Product Owner and Developers, but a change that would endanger the Sprint Goal is not simply inserted into the active Sprint.
    8. The Definition of Done and other quality boundaries are not negotiated away to fit more work into a cycle.
    9. Release decisions determine when completed increments actually reach users.
    10. Formal acceptance still applies where a contract, regulator, or customer requires it separately.

    Five limits matter more than any of the mechanics:

    1. Backlog refinement does not substitute for every organizational or contractual change-control process.
    2. The Product Owner's authority is product authority. It does not automatically extend to budgets, contracts, compliance obligations, personnel, or governance commitments.
    3. A change that endangers the Sprint Goal is renegotiated or deferred, not forced into the running Sprint.
    4. The Scrum Guide defines three accountabilities. The project manager is not a fourth one, even where a project manager legitimately exists alongside the Scrum Team in a larger organization.
    5. Sprint Review feedback is inspection and adaptation, not formal acceptance. Where acceptance is a contractual or regulatory act, it happens through the named acceptance authority.

    Hybrid change routing

    In hybrid delivery the question is not "is this a change?" but "which commitment does it actually touch?" The answer determines the route, and a change can take more than one route at once.

    A proposed change actually affects… Route it to… Records to update
    Backlog order only Product Owner, within delegated product authority Product Backlog
    Work inside the current iteration, with the Sprint Goal intact The Scrum Team accountabilities; Developers and Product Owner renegotiate scope detail Sprint Backlog
    Product Goal or release planning Product Owner with the release or governance decision-maker Product Backlog, release plan, milestone records
    Scope baseline Integrated change control Change request, change log, scope baseline, affected plans
    Schedule baseline Integrated change control Change request, change log, schedule baseline, forecasts
    Cost baseline Integrated change control, and the funding authority where limits are exceeded Change request, change log, cost baseline, funding records
    Contract Procurement and contract authority, through the contract change control system Change request, agreement, procurement records
    Regulatory or compliance commitment Designated governance or compliance authority Change request, compliance evidence, affected baselines
    Architecture Technical or design authority, then integrated change control if a baseline moves Design records, configuration items, change log
    External dependency The dependency owner and the project manager coordinating both sides Issue log, schedule records, agreements
    Transition date Governance, receiving organization, and integrated change control Change request, schedule baseline, transition plan
    Acceptance criteria or formal acceptance conditions Customer or designated acceptance authority Requirements records, acceptance conditions, change log
    Several of the above at once A coordinated decision across the affected authorities Every affected record, updated consistently

    A backlog change is not a baseline change, and calling it one in either direction causes failure. Treating every reordering as integrated change control makes adaptive delivery pointless; treating a baseline effect as "just backlog" removes governance from a commitment someone else is accountable for.

    For ServiceBridge, a stakeholder asks for a clearer status indicator on the case screen. It changes no approved commitment, so the Product Owner orders it in the Product Backlog and the team delivers it in a future Sprint. Separately, a new data-residency requirement arrives. It affects the release date, the hosting contract, the cost baseline, and the customer's acceptance conditions, so it is documented as a change request, analyzed for impact, routed to the change authority, the procurement authority, and the acceptance authority, and — once approved — implemented, verified, and reflected in both the updated baselines and the reordered Product Backlog. The same project uses both routes in the same week without confusing them.

    Approach treatment and exam distinctions

    Predictive control often compares performance with broader baselines. Adaptive control uses frequent review of outcomes, flow, quality, and feedback within governance guardrails. Hybrid control must integrate both evidence types without forcing one cadence to masquerade as the other.

    Data, information, and reports are not interchangeable. Corrective action is not automatic baseline change. Only the authorized role or body may approve a controlled change. Approved change implementation must be verified.

    A metric is not its interpretation, and a visualization is not a decision. The four types of change requests are not interchangeable: corrective action follows a variance that has occurred; preventive action addresses one that has not; defect repair fixes a nonconforming result; an update changes a controlled document. A backlog change is not a baseline change. Product Owner authority is not governance authority. Sprint Review feedback is not formal acceptance, and verification is not acceptance — that decision belongs to Verification, Acceptance, Transition, and Closure.
